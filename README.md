# Applied Stochastic Processes: Modeling, Simulation, and AI

Fourteen implemented modules for a 2-credit graduate course for MS Applied Mathematics and MS Statistics. This course is independent of MATH 6241; neither course requires the other.

## Start here

Read the [online course](https://wanghemath.github.io/AppliedStochasticProcesses/) or [download the student course ZIP](https://github.com/wanghemath/AppliedStochasticProcesses/archive/refs/heads/main.zip) and extract it. Open **index.html** directly in a modern browser. The lessons, interactions, data, and equation renderer are local; no Python web server, network connection, API key, or paid account is required to read and experiment. The browser and Python use different random-number generators. Seed 6231 is reproducible within each implementation, not an assurance of identical cross-language paths. Check distributions and analytical results, not equality of browser/Python streams.

## Data and teaching boundaries

The real-data example is a licensed 365-row subset of UCI Bike Sharing: Fanaee-T, H. (2013), [DOI 10.24432/C5W894](https://doi.org/10.24432/C5W894), under [CC BY 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/). Credit, source README, units, and hashes are preserved in `data/`. The 4,000-rentals/day threshold is a scenario assumption, not measured capacity. All simulated paths are labeled synthetic. Fit uses January–August 2011; September–December is kept in chronological order for validation. Module 3 distinguishes the fitted chain’s stationary law from evidence of stationarity in observed demand, and a stopped first-hit calculation from permanent high-demand absorption. Its four-state operational example is entirely synthetic; it does not estimate actual bike capacity or service outcomes. Module 4 quantifies simulation error conditional on the fitted chain and distinguishes it from parameter and model uncertainty. Its altered transition probabilities are scenario assumptions, and importance-sampling proposals change the simulation distribution, not the target model. September–December is a reused development holdout because it has already been inspected in earlier modules. Modules 3 and 4 form Parts A and B of one Concept Lab 2 due in week 4. Module 5 uses independent Beta priors and the same 242 within-training transitions to calibrate the two Markov hazards. The exact posterior provides a benchmark for Metropolis–Hastings; MCMC is not needed for this conjugate case. Basic split R-hat and the supplied mean MCSE/ESS calculations are teaching diagnostics, not production rank-normalized diagnostics or a convergence guarantee. Posterior parameter uncertainty remains conditional on the homogeneous Markov model and chosen prior; additional draws reduce computational error, not model misspecification. Module 5 starts Part A of Lab 3, completed with Module 6 Part B and submitted once in week 6. Module 6 uses Poisson offspring to compare ensemble growth, finite-horizon extinction, and eventual extinction; deterministic one-child reproduction is explicitly separated from the usual critical extinction claim. A clearly synthetic offspring sample supports a Gamma–Poisson calibration example. The bike connection is a conditional count-model audit: daily completed rentals do not identify generations or offspring and do not support a transport-extinction inference. Module 7 fits constant and working-day calendar rates to January–August daily counts, freezes the estimates, and compares later predictions with persistence on the same target dates. Its Poisson and time-varying-rate simulators use explicitly synthetic event times. The daily data do not identify actual event times or intraday peaks; residual dispersion and temporal structure diagnose model limitations. Module 7 starts Lab 4 Part A, completed with Module 8 Part B and submitted once in week 8. Module 8 completes Lab 4 Part B with renewal memory, a finite birth–death chain, and a daily-count exposure and measurement audit. Its bike-station capacity, return rate, and rental-attempt rate are synthetic scenario parameters; daily system totals cannot identify these quantities or station holding times. Time-weighted occupation and jump-visit frequencies answer different questions. The observed daily exposure, rental totals, means, and zero-count days are summarized separately before and after the chronological split. The data contain no station identifiers, inventory, returns, rental attempts, or event timestamps, so they do not identify renewal gaps or continuous-time station rates. Module 9 introduces a single-server first-come, first-served queue and verifies an event calendar against Lindley’s recursion, stable M/M/1 and M/D/1 formulas, and exact finite-path queue/system area identities. Warm-up customer deletion and finite-cohort drainage are stated explicitly; MCSE uses independent replication means, not dependent customer waits. Bursty arrivals and overload expose benchmark limits. The January–August observed mean supplies a volume scale for an assumed maintenance referral fraction and shift length; neither maintenance arrivals nor service rates are fitted. Two service-rate scenarios use the January–August volume scale for conditional queue calculations. The later-period mean provides a descriptive comparison of observed volume; neither analysis validates real waiting-time predictions. Module 9 begins Lab 5 Part A, combined with Module 10 Part B for one submission in week 10. The existing model/validation checkpoint remains due in week 9. No additional project is introduced. Module 10 completes Lab 5 Part B by checking Brownian increment scaling, quadratic variation, and exact geometric Brownian motion against Euler–Maruyama using shared increments on nested grids. It separates strong error, paired mean error, Monte Carlo uncertainty, and model discrepancy; a coarse high-volatility example exposes nonpositive Euler paths. The real-data example fits a continuous log-growth proxy to the 242 January–August increments and freezes its parameters for 122 rolling one-day forecasts using actual previous-day observations. Conditional means are compared with persistence and 80% interval coverage is reported. The daily completed-rental counts are not a physical diffusion; negative lag-one dependence and temporal change challenge the independent, stationary log-increment assumption. The later observations remain a reused development holdout, not a fresh test.

Module 11 begins Lab 6 Part A, combined with Module 12 Part B for one submission in week 12. It compares RBF and exponential covariance assumptions, verifies Gaussian conditioning using Cholesky solves, and separates latent-function uncertainty from the uncertainty in new noisy observations. For the bike demonstration, the mean and population-standard-deviation scale (ddof=0) use only the 243 January–August counts. RBF amplitude 1, length scales 14 and 45 days, and observation-noise standard deviation 0.5 in standardized units are prespecified teaching settings, not estimates tuned on later observations. All 122 September–December forecasts originate on August 31, without updates or refitting; training-mean and last-training-value baselines use that same information. The fixed-origin protocol differs from Module 10’s rolling forecasts, so their aggregate scores should not be compared as a model ranking. Intervals condition on plug-in mean, scale, and kernel settings, omit their estimation uncertainty, and do not guarantee empirical coverage. Gaussian observations approximate integer counts and can allow negative predictions. Later data remain a reused development period; inspecting two settings there does not create a fresh final test. No additional project is introduced.

Module 12 completes Lab 6 Part B, submitted once with Module 11 in week 12. It compares rolling one-day forecasts under one shared information protocol: January–June supplies 181 initial-history days, July–August supplies 62 selection targets, and September–December supplies 122 reused assessment targets. A GP is reconditioned at each origin on the previous 60 observed counts, with mean and population SD estimated inside that earlier window only. RBF amplitude 1 and noise SD 0.5 remain prespecified in standardized units; July–August MAE selects between length scales 14 and 45 days, then that choice is frozen. Persistence and weekly-lag baselines use the same target dates, with predictive residual scales estimated only from the past 60 baseline errors. A 4,500-completed-rentals threshold is a new explicit planning assumption, not measured capacity. MAE, RMSE, Gaussian negative log predictive density, Brier score, and central 80% interval coverage, width, and interval score answer complementary questions. Multiplying predictive SD by 0.5, 1, or 2 shows how interval and density scores respond without changing point forecasts. A paired block bootstrap illustrates loss-comparison uncertainty under dependence assumptions; block-length sensitivity and temporal change limit its interpretation. Leakage is demonstrated as a deliberately invalid forecast. The later period has been reused across the course; chronological separation within this exercise does not make it a pristine final test. This protocol differs from Module 11’s fixed origin, so scores from the two modules cannot directly rank their models. No extra project or lab is added.

Module 13 refines the same sustained project and adds no concept lab. Its worked audit revisits the two-state bike chain at the declared 4,000-rentals threshold: 242 January–August transitions estimate a frozen matrix, and 122 later rolling one-day forecasts are compared with persistence using Brier scores. Exact seven-step probabilities provide a benchmark for conditional simulations; resetting a seed inside each path exposes a reproducible but invalid independence claim. Changes to the low-to-high transition probability are declared sensitivity scenarios, not causal effects or measured capacity changes. This is an audit of one model component, not a replacement for the project’s two or three meaningful methods. It records the source data, checksum, units, chronological information boundary, model assumptions, environment, and random-number setup; analytical benchmarks and independent invariants check the computations. Seeded reruns establish repeatability within a stated implementation, not universal bitwise agreement or model validity. The bike counts remain observed systemwide completed rentals, and later dates remain reused development evidence. Deliberately flawed code and claims support an optional AI-output audit that needs no account; students record consequential assistance, checks, corrections, and unresolved limitations and must explain the submitted analysis. Use the existing week 13 project allocation to refine the decision brief and rehearse the individual explanation.

Module 14 closes the course by presenting and explaining the same sustained project; it introduces no new required stochastic method, concept lab, or project. The bike-sharing worked example traces the existing two-state chain from measured counts and fitted transitions to exact conditional probabilities, a calculated simulation-precision reference, and reused chronological validation. Students connect each claim to its units, information boundary, computation, uncertainty, and limitation; a hypothetical transition change remains a sensitivity scenario, not a causal capacity effect. A six-minute presentation followed by questions and an individual formula, figure, and code explanation rehearse the existing deliverables: executed notebook, 4–5 page report, one-page decision brief, and slides. Consequential AI-style claims are audited without requiring a live AI account. The final project remains 30% of the course grade; individual explanation remains 6 percentage points within the separate 10% explanation-and-participation component. The six concept labs, Lab 6 due in week 12, and the week 9 checkpoint are unchanged.

MATH 6241 lecture source files in the adjacent course folder were inspected as conceptual references. Private instructor notes record exact files and section mappings. The reference notes and their solutions were not copied into student materials. Student/grade records were not used.
