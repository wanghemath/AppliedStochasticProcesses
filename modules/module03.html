03 / Long-run behavior & first passage

What happens eventually—and how long does it take?

A high-rental day tomorrow, the fraction of high days over many years, and the wait until the next high day are different quantities. A Markov model gives each a precise meaning.

40–50 min guided work25–35 min notebook practiceConcept Lab 2 · Part A

01 / Model the question“Eventually” needs a definition.

Continue with $Y_t=\mathbf{1}\{X_t>4000\}$, where $X_t$ is the observed number of completed bike rentals on day $t$. State 0 means at most 4,000 rentals; state 1 means more than 4,000. The threshold is a scenario choice, not measured fleet capacity. Our row convention remains $\nu_{t+h}=\nu_tP^h$.

Long-run composition

Is there a probability vector that a transition leaves unchanged? Do forecasts from a given start approach it? Does one long path spend that fraction of time in each state?

Time to an event

Starting on a low day, how long until the first high day? In a separate operational scenario, is an issue eventually resolved or escalated, and how many steps does that take?

By the end: solve and check a stationary law, state the conditions for convergence, distinguish first hits from returns, retain censored paths, and compute absorption probabilities and mean times with a transient submatrix.

Predict before computing

Use the ideas from Module 2: conditional transitions, matrix powers, simulation across independent paths, and training-only estimation.

02 / MathematicsInvariant does not automatically mean attracting.

A stationary distribution is a nonnegative row vector $\pi$ satisfying

$$\pi P=\pi,\qquad \sum_i\pi_i=1.$$

If $Y_0\sim\pi$, then $Y_t\sim\pi$ for every $t$. The states along a path may still be dependent.

For the two-state chain below, balance gives $\pi_0a=\pi_1b$. Combining it with normalization yields

$$P=\begin{pmatrix}1-a&a\\b&1-b\end{pmatrix},\qquad \pi=\left(\frac{b}{a+b},\frac{a}{a+b}\right),\quad a+b>0.$$

For $a=0.2,b=0.3$, $\pi=(0.6,0.4)$. Verify by multiplying on the left: $(0.6,0.4)P=(0.6,0.4)$. The vector $(1,1)^\mathsf{T}$ is a right eigenvector of every row-stochastic matrix; normalizing it usually does not give the stationary probabilities.

Finite-state conclusions and their conditions
QuestionSufficient conditionsConclusion
Does a stationary law exist?Any finite stochastic matrixAt least one exists.
Is it unique?Finite and irreducible: every state can reach every otherExactly one stationary law.
Do endpoint laws converge?Finite, irreducible and aperiodic: returns have no forced common cycle length greater than 1$\nu_0P^n\to\pi$ from every initial law.
Do time averages converge?Finite and irreducible; periodicity is allowed$n^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^n\mathbf{1}\{Y_t=i\}\to\pi_i$ almost surely.

These are sufficient conditions. A reducible chain with one absorbing state can also have a unique stationary law and marginal convergence. Two boundary examples sharpen the distinction: the alternating chain has a unique invariant law $(1/2,1/2)$ but a fixed-state start oscillates; the identity matrix has every probability vector stationary, while each path stays at its starting state.

04 / Observed dataA stationary fitted chain is not evidence of stationary rentals.

Reuse the unsmoothed Module 2 fit: Jan 1–Aug 31 supplies 243 days and 242 adjacent pairs, all wholly inside training. No later outcomes enter this matrix.

$$N=\begin{pmatrix}114&20\\19&89\end{pmatrix},\quad \widehat P=\begin{pmatrix}114/134&20/134\\19/108&89/108\end{pmatrix},\quad \widehat\pi_1=\frac{1080}{2353}\approx0.4590.$$

The matrix is irreducible and aperiodic, so its forecasts converge. That theorem describes the fitted homogeneous model. It does not prove that the observed rental process has constant transitions across seasons.

05 / First passageCount until the first visit, and keep unfinished paths.

For a target set $A$, define the first hitting time

$$T_A=\inf\{n\ge0:Y_n\in A\},\qquad \inf\varnothing=\infty.$$

Starting inside $A$ gives $T_A=0$. A first return time uses $n\ge1$ and asks a different question.

Starting from low state 0 and targeting $A=\{1\}$, each continued low day leaves the same chance $a=20/134$ of reaching high next day under the fitted model. Thus $T_A$ is geometric on $1,2,\ldots$:

$$\Pr_0(T_A>n)=(1-a)^n,\qquad \Pr_0(T_A\le H)=1-(1-a)^H,\qquad \mathbb E_0[T_A]=1/a=6.7\text{ days}.$$

You may replace the target's outgoing row with an absorbing row to compute this first-passage event. This is a stopping device; the actual bike chain can return from high to low. The seven-day endpoint probability $[\widehat P^7]_{01}$ and the probability of at least one high day by day 7 are not the same.

A simulation horizon is not the full waiting time.

If a path has no hit through day $H$, its hitting time is right-censored. Store both $\min(T_A,H)$ and the indicator $\mathbf1\{T_A\le H\}$. A hit exactly at $H$ is observed, although its capped time equals that of a censored path. Averaging only completed hits estimates a conditional mean and biases the estimate of the full mean downward.

$$\mathbb E[\min(T_A,H)]=\sum_{n=0}^{H-1}\Pr(T_A>n)=\frac{1-(1-a)^H}{a}\quad(a>0,\ Y_0=0).$$

This restricted mean is a valid finite-horizon target. It is not the full mean unless the tail beyond $H$ contributes nothing.

Optional distinction: first return to high

For a finite irreducible chain, the mean first return time to state $i$, starting in $i$ and counting from step 1, is $1/\pi_i$. For high rental days this model gives $1/\widehat\pi_1\approx2.1787$ days. This is neither the 6.7-day first hit from low nor $T_{\{1\}}=0$ from high under the step-zero definition.

07 / Absorbing chainsSeparate the outcome from the time to that outcome.

Now use an entirely synthetic operational example. States 0 and 1 mean “routine” and “strained”; states 2 and 3 mean “resolved” and “escalated.” The terminal states are absorbing for this scenario. These meanings and transition probabilities are assumptions, not estimates of bike capacity or service performance.

$$P_{\rm abs}=\begin{pmatrix}0.6&0.2&0.2&0\\0.1&0.5&0&0.4\\0&0&1&0\\0&0&0&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}Q&R\\0&I\end{pmatrix}.$$

If all states represented by $Q$ are transient, its spectral radius is less than 1. The fundamental matrix $F$, absorption probabilities $B$, and expected time vector $\boldsymbol t$ satisfy

$$F=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}Q^n=(I-Q)^{-1},\qquad B=FR,\qquad \boldsymbol t=F\mathbf1.$$

$F_{ij}$ counts expected visits to transient state $j$ before absorption, including time 0 when $i=j$. $B_{ik}$ is the eventual probability of terminal outcome $k$ from transient state $i$.

First-step reasoning gives $B=R+QB$ and $\boldsymbol t=\mathbf1+Q\boldsymbol t$. Use a linear solve and check these residuals. Here $\rho(Q)=0.7$,

$$F=\begin{pmatrix}25/9&10/9\\5/9&20/9\end{pmatrix},\quad B=\begin{pmatrix}5/9&4/9\\1/9&8/9\end{pmatrix},\quad \boldsymbol t=\begin{pmatrix}35/9\\25/9\end{pmatrix}.$$

From routine, resolution has probability $5/9$ and the expected wait to either absorbing outcome is $35/9\approx3.8889$ steps. This is not a conditional mean time to resolution. For a general target $A$, hitting means obey $t_i=1+\sum_{j\notin A}P_{ij}t_j$, for $i\notin A$, with $t_i=0$ on $A$, only when the relevant means are finite. Check reachability and transience before solving.

08 / Verification & interpretationTest the claim before trusting the number.

Verify the mathematics and code

Check $\pi P=\pi$, normalization, nonnegativity, simulated endpoint frequencies, $T_A=0$ on target, unreachable targets, and hits exactly at $H$. Check $(I-Q)F=I$, $(I-Q)B=R$, $(I-Q)\boldsymbol t=\mathbf1$, and absorption row sums when absorption is certain.

Validate the model against data

Compare monthly fractions and complete-window first-hit outcomes against frozen predictions. Keep the fitting and evaluation dates explicit. A small algebraic residual says nothing about seasonal changes or whether two states retain enough information.

Three uncertainties remain distinct: path variability with a known model; Monte Carlo error from a finite number of simulated paths; and uncertainty in fitted transitions and model assumptions. Increasing $R$ addresses only Monte Carlo error. Dependent time averages and overlapping observed windows require dependence-aware inference beyond this module.

This instructor-written flawed assistant-style proposal can be audited without an AI account:
