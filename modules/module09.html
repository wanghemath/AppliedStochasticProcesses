09 / Queueing systems & discrete-event simulation

How much capacity
prevents long waits?

A server can be faster than the average incoming workload and still create long delays. Build an event calendar, verify its accounting, and learn how utilization and variability change the waiting experience.

45–55 min guided work25–35 min notebook practiceConcept Lab 5 · Part A

01 / Practical questionCan a repair desk keep up with incoming jobs?

A bike-maintenance desk accepts jobs, serves one at a time, and lets the others wait. A model can compare faster service with more regular service, but those interventions affect different parts of the queue. An average workload below capacity is a stability condition, not a promise of short waits.

Predict before running

The event times and service durations in this lesson are synthetic. The observed bike-sharing counts provide a scale for an explicitly assumed maintenance scenario, not measurements of arrivals to a repair desk. Module 8’s station model blocked impossible rentals or returns; this module assumes an infinite waiting room and no lost jobs.

By the end: define a queue and its units, use M/M/1 and M/D/1 benchmarks with their assumptions, implement a next-event calendar, verify it against Lindley recursion and path-area identities, quantify replication error, and compare capacity scenarios without presenting assumptions as data estimates.

02 / Define the queueSeparate arrivals, service, and the waiting discipline.

Assume one work-conserving server: it is busy whenever a job is present. Jobs receive first-come, first-served (FCFS) service, with an unlimited buffer, no abandonment, no preemption, and no rejection. The queue starts empty. Service requirements are iid and independent of the arrival gaps.

Here $N(t)$ is a queue population, not Module 7’s cumulative event count. With one server, $Q(t)=\max\{N(t)-1,0\}$ and $W_i=W_{q,i}+S_i$. The mean service duration is $1/\mu$; NumPy’s exponential sampler takes that scale, not the rate $\mu$.

Kendall notation identifies arrival law / service law / server count: M/M/1 uses Poisson arrivals and exponential service; M/D/1 uses Poisson arrivals and fixed service; a renewal arrival law with exponential service gives a GI/M/1 queue. The shorthand alone does not specify every buffer or discipline assumption, so we state those separately.

$$\rho=\lambda\mathbb E[S]=\frac\lambda\mu.$$

For the stochastic arrival and service families used here, $\rho<1$ permits a stationary queue with finite mean delays. At $\rho\geq1$ there is no finite stationary mean delay. At strict overload, $\rho>1$, incoming workload exceeds service capacity in the long run. At the critical boundary, avoid claiming that every sample path grows monotonically.

A finite run can finish even when a queue is unstable. Our simulator admits a finite cohort and then stops arrivals. Every job has a finite service duration, so the cohort drains. That termination says nothing about stability under arrivals that continue forever.

03 / Exact benchmarksDelay grows sharply near full utilization.

For a stationary M/M/1 queue with $\lambda<\mu$, the birth–death model has upward rate $\lambda$ and downward rate $\mu$ whenever the system is nonempty. Its stationary population is geometric:

$$\pi_n=(1-\rho)\rho^n,\quad n=0,1,\ldots,\qquad L=\frac\rho{1-\rho},\qquad L_q=\frac{\rho^2}{1-\rho}.$$

Little’s law then connects mean population and time: $L=\lambda\mathbb E[W]$ and $L_q=\lambda\mathbb E[W_q]$. In this stable lossless queue, long-run departure rate equals arrival rate.

$$\mathbb E[W]=\frac1{\mu-\lambda},\qquad \mathbb E[W_q]=\frac\lambda{\mu(\mu-\lambda)}.$$

At $\lambda=2$/hour and $\mu=3$/hour, utilization is $2/3$, expected queue wait is $2/3$ hour, and expected total time is one hour. The server’s mean service duration is only one-third hour. Confusing service, queue wait, and total time changes the answer by large factors.

Keep the service mean and change its variability

For Poisson arrivals, iid independent service times with finite second moment, FCFS, one server, an unlimited buffer, and $\rho<1$, the Pollaczek–Khinchine mean formula is

$$\mathbb E[W_q]=\frac{\lambda\mathbb E[S^2]}{2(1-\rho)},\qquad \mathbb E[W]=\mathbb E[W_q]+\frac1\mu.$$

Exponential service has $\mathbb E[S^2]=2/\mu^2$. Fixed service has $\mathbb E[S^2]=1/\mu^2$, so M/D/1 halves the mean queue wait at the same arrival rate and mean service time. At the defaults, its mean queue wait is one-third hour and total time is two-thirds hour. This formula is not a general GI/G/1 formula. Larson–Odoni, §4.7 provides the supplemental M/G/1 reference.

The “bursty renewal” option below uses independent gaps: with probability one-half draw an exponential gap with mean $0.5/\lambda$, otherwise use mean $1.5/\lambda$. Its overall mean is $1/\lambda$ and squared coefficient of variation is $1.5$, versus 1 for Poisson arrivals. It creates more variable gaps, not a persistent time-varying intensity regime. The M/M/1 and Poisson-arrival mean formulas do not apply to that option; no exact delay benchmark for it is supplied in this lesson.

04 / Worked computationAdvance to the next event, not the next time step.

A discrete-event simulation stores the next arrival and next departure. Between events, queue size is constant. Jump directly to the earlier event, integrate the old state over that interval, and update the queue. No fixed time grid is needed.

  1. Start empty at time zero, with no scheduled departure.
  2. At an arrival, add the job to the FCFS line. If the server was idle, begin service and schedule its completion.
  3. At a departure, remove the completed job. If another job is waiting, start its service immediately and schedule the next completion.
  4. After the last arrival, continue processing departures until every admitted job finishes.

For ties, process a departure before an arrival. Simultaneous arrivals keep their input order. This makes deterministic checks reproducible; continuous random laws almost surely avoid ties. The notebook uses two clocks because this model needs only one pending arrival and one pending departure. More complex systems can store an event list in a priority queue.

next_time = min(next_arrival, next_departure)
area_number += number_in_system * (next_time - time)
area_queue += max(number_in_system - 1, 0) * (next_time - time)
time = next_time

An independent route: Lindley’s recursion

Write $C_i$ for service start time, set $D_0=0$, and give each job its service requirement before simulation. Then

$$C_i=\max\{A_i,D_{i-1}\},\qquad D_i=C_i+S_i,\qquad W_{q,i}=C_i-A_i.$$

Equivalently, $W_{q,i+1}=\max\{0,W_{q,i}+S_i-(A_{i+1}-A_i)\}$. This recursion provides a separate implementation against which to check the event calendar on identical input arrays.

06 / Accounting & simulation accuracyUse the same customers and the same time window.

For the full cohort of $n=B+N$ jobs, observed from the empty start at zero until its last departure $H$, the event calendar gives exact sample-path identities:

$$\int_0^H Q(t)\,dt=\sum_{i=1}^n W_{q,i},\qquad \int_0^H N(t)\,dt=\sum_{i=1}^n W_i.$$

Divide by $H$. With realized throughput $\widehat\lambda_H=n/H$ and full-cohort mean delays,

$$\widehat L_{q,H}=\widehat\lambda_H\overline W_{q,\mathrm{all}},\qquad \widehat L_H=\widehat\lambda_H\overline W_{\mathrm{all}}.$$

These finite identities hold even for a cohort drawn from an overloaded model. Their throughput is the realized $n/H$, not necessarily the input rate $\lambda$. They use all customers, including the discarded initialization cohort, and do not substitute the retained-cohort means from the experiment table.

CheckComputed valuesInterpretation

If observation instead stops at a fixed time while customers remain, some system times are right censored. The path area counts each customer’s overlap with the observation window. It does not equal the sum of completed customers’ entire system times. Do not delete unfinished customers and call the result an unbiased mean delay.

Customers in one queue are dependent

Let $Y_r$ be the retained-cohort mean queue wait in replica $r$. Independent complete replications give

$$\overline Y=\frac1R\sum_{r=1}^R Y_r,\qquad \widehat{\operatorname{MCSE}}(\overline Y)=\frac{s_Y}{\sqrt R}.$$

This quantifies random error for the specified finite experiment. Adjacent customers share congestion, so treating all customer waits as iid can seriously understate error. An approximate interval can use $\overline Y\pm t_{R-1,0.975}s_Y/\sqrt R$ if the distribution of replica means is sufficiently regular; it does not include model error or initialization bias.

Starting empty often gives short early delays. Compare $B=0$, $1000$, and $5000$ and increase retained run length, especially near $\rho=1$. No fixed warmup count proves stationarity. Increasing replicas narrows Monte Carlo error while leaving systematic initialization effects intact. Across scenarios, a common seed can aid reproducibility, but a paired variance-reduction claim requires deliberately aligned random inputs and an MCSE for paired differences.

Consider a hypothetical maintenance intake desk. Assume $p$ maintenance referrals per completed rental, distributed over eight operating hours per day. Use only the training-period volume to define a constant-rate scenario:

$$\lambda^*=\frac{p\,\overline C_{\mathrm{train}}}{8\ \text{hours/day}},\qquad \overline C_{\mathrm{train}}=\frac{802684}{243}\ \text{rentals/day}.$$

At $p=0.005$, this gives $\lambda^*\approx2.064516$ jobs/hour. The referral fraction, eight-hour schedule, Poisson arrivals, exponential service, and service rates are all assumptions. Converting a daily mean into an hourly rate does not establish a homogeneous Poisson arrival process. This stationary scenario treats operating time as continuous and omits daily opening/closing transients and overnight policies.

Two assumed service capacities

Both scenarios use one server; μ=3/hour means a 20-minute mean service, μ=4/hour means 15 minutes. These rates are not fitted to the rental data.
