10 / Brownian motion & numerical stochastic dynamics

How does
noise accumulate?

A smaller time step should reveal finer fluctuations, not erase them. Learn the square-root scaling of Brownian noise, simulate positive random growth exactly, and measure what a numerical approximation changes.

45–55 min guided work25–35 min notebook practiceConcept Lab 5 · Part B

01 / Practical questionDoes a finer simulation preserve the same uncertainty?

Suppose an evolving demand level experiences many small random changes. An additive model allows positive and negative fluctuations; a multiplicative model scales noise with the current level. Neither model becomes appropriate simply because its simulated paths look plausible.

Predict before running

We first use synthetic Brownian and geometric Brownian paths with known benchmarks. We then fit a deliberately limited continuous-valued proxy to the same observed bike counts and compare its one-day predictions with persistence. This lets implementation verification and empirical model validation lead to different conclusions.

By the end: construct Brownian grid increments, check their variance and quadratic variation, distinguish GBM log drift from SDE drift, compare exact and Euler paths using shared noise, separate discretization bias from Monte Carlo error, and assess a diffusion proxy with a chronological forecast comparison.

02 / Brownian scalingNoise over a short interval has standard deviation √h.

Standard Brownian motion $W(t)$ starts at zero, has almost surely continuous paths, and has independent increments over disjoint intervals. Its increments are stationary and Gaussian:

$$W(t+h)-W(t)\sim N(0,h),\qquad \mathbb E[W(t)]=0,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(W(t))=t,\qquad \operatorname{Cov}(W(s),W(t))=\min(s,t).$$

For a grid $t_j=jh$, $h=T/n$, draw independent standard normals $Z_j$ and set

$$\Delta W_j=\sqrt h\,Z_j,\qquad W(t_j)=\sum_{k=1}^j\Delta W_k.$$

These are exact joint Brownian values at the selected grid times. Straight segments between them are visual interpolation; they do not sample the unobserved Brownian fluctuations or extrema inside each interval. Brownian paths are continuous but almost surely nowhere differentiable, so a finite-difference slope is not an ordinary velocity.

h = T / n
increments = np.sqrt(h) * rng.normal(size=(replicas, n))
paths = np.column_stack([np.zeros(replicas), increments.cumsum(axis=1)])
# Use independent rows for endpoint statistics.

Roughness survives refinement

The grid quadratic variation is $V_n=\sum_{j=1}^n(\Delta W_j)^2$. For equal Brownian steps,

$$\mathbb E[V_n]=T,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(V_n)=\frac{2T^2}{n},\qquad V_n\longrightarrow T\ \text{in mean square as }n\to\infty.$$

If you mistakenly use $hZ_j$, terminal variance and expected quadratic variation both become $Th=T^2/n$ and vanish under refinement. A smoother-looking plot can therefore be a scaling error. The grid variance of $V_n$ for this wrong construction is $2n h^4$.

03 / Positive random growthUse Brownian noise multiplicatively.

Geometric Brownian motion (GBM) solves the Itô stochastic differential equation

$$dS_t=\mu S_t\,dt+\sigma S_t\,dW_t,\qquad S_0>0,\quad \sigma\geq0.$$

$\mu$ is the SDE drift, with units inverse time; $\sigma$ has units inverse square root of time. The noise integral is an Itô stochastic integral, not ordinary multiplication by a differentiable $W_t$. For constant coefficients, the exact solution is

$$S_t=S_0\exp\!\left[(\mu-\tfrac12\sigma^2)t+\sigma W_t\right].$$

The $-\sigma^2/2$ correction is essential. The mean log change per unit time is $m=\mu-\sigma^2/2$, while expected level growth uses $\mu$. The supplied Brownian-motion reference writes GBM using a log-drift parameter; translate that parameter to $m$ here rather than identifying it with the SDE drift.

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbb E[S_t]&=S_0e^{\mu t},\\ \operatorname{Var}(S_t)&=S_0^2e^{2\mu t}(e^{\sigma^2t}-1),\\ \operatorname{median}(S_t)&=S_0e^{(\mu-\sigma^2/2)t}.\end{aligned}$$

With $S_0=1$, $\mu=0.2$, $\sigma=0.8$, and $T=1$ synthetic time unit, the exact terminal mean is 1.221403, variance 1.337392, and median 0.886920. A mean above the starting level can coexist with a median below it because occasional large values pull up the mean.

The exact multiplicative grid update is $S_{j+1}=S_j\exp[(\mu-\sigma^2/2)h+\sigma\Delta W_j]$. It preserves positivity and has no discretization error at the grid points for this particular constant-coefficient model. A general stochastic differential equation need not have such an exact update.

04 / Numerical stochastic dynamicsApproximate the equation using the same noise.

For an Itô equation $dX_t=a(X_t)dt+b(X_t)dW_t$, Euler–Maruyama approximates one step by

$$X_{j+1}^{\mathrm{EM}}=X_j^{\mathrm{EM}}+a(X_j^{\mathrm{EM}})h+b(X_j^{\mathrm{EM}})\Delta W_j.$$

For GBM this becomes $S_{j+1}^{\mathrm{EM}}=S_j^{\mathrm{EM}}(1+\mu h+\sigma\Delta W_j)$. A normal increment can make this factor negative. Clipping such values to zero changes the numerical method and its distribution; it is not an accuracy correction.

dw = np.sqrt(h) * rng.normal(size=n)
w = np.r_[0.0, np.cumsum(dw)]
exact = S0 * np.exp((mu - sigma**2 / 2) * times + sigma * w)
euler = np.empty(n + 1)
euler[0] = S0
for j in range(n):
    euler[j + 1] = euler[j] * (1 + mu*h + sigma*dw[j])

Both paths must use the same increments to measure pathwise numerical error. Comparing independent exact and Euler simulations mostly measures different Brownian realizations. To compare time steps, generate the finest increments once and sum consecutive blocks to construct every coarser increment.

With σ=0, GBM reduces to the deterministic ODE $dS=\mu Sdt$. Euler still has ordinary time-step error unless the coefficients make the update exact. With μ=0 and σ>0, the Euler terminal mean is exactly unbiased while pathwise error remains nonzero.

05 / Two kinds of errorA small mean bias can hide a large pathwise error.

For matched exact and Euler paths at step size h, let $D_r(h)=S^{\mathrm{EM},(r)}_T-S^{\mathrm{exact},(r)}_T$. The terminal root-mean-square error estimates a strong error:

$$\operatorname{RMSE}_{\mathrm{strong}}(h)=\sqrt{\mathbb E[D(h)^2]},\qquad \widehat{\operatorname{RMSE}}(h)=\sqrt{\frac1R\sum_{r=1}^R D_r(h)^2}.$$

The paired mean error estimates the weak bias for the identity payoff $f(S)=S$: $\mathbb E[S_T^{\mathrm{EM}}]-\mathbb E[S_T]$. It uses the same differences but averages before squaring:

$$\overline D(h)=\frac1R\sum_{r=1}^R D_r(h),\qquad \widehat{\operatorname{MCSE}}(\overline D)=\frac{s_D}{\sqrt R}.$$

Under suitable coefficient and payoff regularity, Euler–Maruyama typically has strong order $1/2$ and weak order 1. These are asymptotic statements, not guarantees for every stochastic equation or every finite mesh. For this smooth GBM example, refining h by a factor four roughly halves strong RMS error. A particular weak functional can have zero bias, as the mean does when μ=0.

The exact finite-step Euler mean is $S_0(1+\mu h)^n$, so its mean bias is $S_0[(1+\mu h)^n-e^{\mu T}]$. At the default parameters, n=256 gives bias about −0.00009537. The estimated mean difference can be dominated by sampling error at this resolution. More paths reduce MCSE; smaller steps reduce discretization error. Neither operation repairs a misspecified model.

How the independent RMS and positivity benchmarks are calculated

For n=T/h, independence of normal increments gives the mixed moment and Euler second moment

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbb E[(S_T^{\mathrm{EM}})^2]&=S_0^2[(1+\mu h)^2+\sigma^2h]^n,\\ \mathbb E[S_T^{\mathrm{exact}}S_T^{\mathrm{EM}}]&=S_0^2e^{\mu T}[1+(\mu+\sigma^2)h]^n.\end{aligned}$$

Combine these with $\mathbb E[(S_T^{\mathrm{exact}})^2]=S_0^2e^{(2\mu+\sigma^2)T}$ to compute $\mathbb E[D^2]$. For σ>0, a multiplier is negative with probability $p_h=\Phi(-(1+\mu h)/(\sigma\sqrt h))$. Independent multipliers give probability $1-(1-p_h)^n$ of ever being nonpositive and $[1-(1-2p_h)^n]/2$ of a negative endpoint. Exact equality to zero has probability zero for nondegenerate Gaussian multipliers. These are numerical-method properties, not negative values of the exact GBM.

Numerical-method reference: Higham (2001), An Algorithmic Introduction to Numerical Simulation of Stochastic Differential Equations. The notebook uses NumPy and Matplotlib; no specialized SDE package is needed.

06 / A real-data model trialTest a diffusion proxy against observed bike demand.

The unchanged UCI dataset contains 365 observed 2011 daily system-wide completed-rental counts. They are positive integers and daily aggregates, not direct observations of a continuous GBM state or instantaneous rental intensity. We deliberately try a continuous-valued positive proxy for daily demand levels, then assess its limitations.

Let $C_t$ denote completed rentals on day t. With one day as the time unit, compute the 242 within-training log changes $r_t=\log(C_t/C_{t-1})$ from January–August only. The candidate model assumes these are iid normal with daily mean m and variance v. Its maximum-likelihood estimates are

$$\widehat m=\frac1{242}\sum r_t,\qquad \widehat v=\frac1{242}\sum(r_t-\widehat m)^2,\qquad \widehat\sigma=\sqrt{\widehat v},\qquad \widehat\mu=\widehat m+\tfrac12\widehat v.$$

The denominator is 242 for the normal-model variance MLE, not 241. These formulas use Δt=1 day: m and μ are per day and σ is per √day. All bundled counts are positive; no pseudocount or zero filtering is applied. Data with zero counts would require a different observation/model treatment before taking logs.

$$\begin{aligned}\widehat C_{t+1,\mathrm{mean}}&=C_t e^{\widehat\mu},&\widehat C_{t+1,\mathrm{median}}&=C_t e^{\widehat m},\\ [L_{t+1},U_{t+1}]&=C_t\exp\!\left(\widehat m\pm z_{0.9}\widehat\sigma\right),&z_{0.9}&\approx1.28155.\end{aligned}$$

The mean is the squared-error point target; the median is the absolute-error point target under the assumed conditional law. Compare both fairly with persistence, $\widehat C_{t+1}=C_t$, on the same 122 target dates. These are rolling one-day forecasts, not a single August-31 forecast recursively propagated for four months.

07 / Model checksA working solver can support a poor data model.

The training log changes have lag-one correlation about −0.3795. This descriptive dependence diagnostic challenges the candidate iid-increment assumption. Inspect the time series and log-change distribution as well; one correlation is not a complete model check. Observed calendar patterns, count variation, and changing demand levels can require a different model or explicit covariates.

What the numerical checks establish

The Brownian generator has the correct variance scaling. Coupled Euler paths approach the exact GBM solution as the step shrinks. The formulas, seed replay, and parameter transformations agree across implementations.

What the bike comparison establishes

This fitted mean forecast loses to persistence on the reused later period. The data question independent log changes. They do not measure continuous latent states, Brownian driving noise, within-day variability, unmet demand, or station capacity.

Project decision: keep or reject the diffusion proxy based on the measured target, baseline comparison, diagnostics, and intended forecast horizon. A constant fitted drift should not be extrapolated into indefinite growth. A useful result can be a documented failure of this model family, within the existing sustained project. Do not tune repeatedly on September–December and then describe it as an untouched test set.
