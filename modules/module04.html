04 / Simulation accuracy & variance reduction

How much simulation is enough?

A stable-looking estimate can still be wrong. Measure simulation error, make rare events easier to sample, and compare scenarios using the same randomness.

40–50 min guided work25–35 min notebook practiceConcept Lab 2 · Part B

01 / Practical questionHow often could a low-rental period persist?

Retain $Y_t=\mathbf1\{X_t>4000\}$ from Modules 2–3, where $X_t$ is the observed daily number of completed bike rentals. From state 0, let $T=\inf\{n\ge0:Y_n=1\}$ be the first high day. Our target is the probability of no high day during the next $H=30$ days, conditional on the frozen fitted model and a low starting day.

$$a=\widehat P_{01}=20/134,\qquad p_H=\Pr_a(T>H)=(1-a)^H,\qquad p_{30}\approx0.0078343.$$

This event is the complement of hitting high by $H$; it is not the endpoint event $Y_H=0$. The fitted model makes it rare. We know the exact answer here, so this is a testbed for methods we can later use when exact answers are unavailable.

Predict before computing

By the end: distinguish Monte Carlo error from model error; use independent replications to estimate standard errors; construct and verify an importance-sampling estimator; and compute a paired standard error for a scenario comparison.

02 / Essential mathematicsMeasure variation across independent replications.

Let $Z_r$ be the output of replication $r$, with the same distribution and independent across $r$. For crude Monte Carlo, $Z_r=\mathbf1\{T_r>H\}$. With finite variance and $R\ge2$:

$$\widehat\mu_R=\frac1R\sum_{r=1}^R Z_r,\qquad s_Z^2=\frac1{R-1}\sum_{r=1}^R(Z_r-\widehat\mu_R)^2,\qquad \widehat{\operatorname{MCSE}}=\frac{s_Z}{\sqrt R}.$$

The Monte Carlo standard error estimates the standard deviation of the estimator over repeated simulation experiments. It differs from $s_Z$, the standard deviation of individual outputs. For Bernoulli outputs, the exact model MCSE is $\sqrt{p_H(1-p_H)/R}$. We use that known value only as a verification benchmark.

$$\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\mu_R)=\operatorname{Var}(Z)/R,\qquad \widehat\mu_R\pm1.96\,s_Z/\sqrt R\quad\text{(approximate 95\% normal interval).}$$

Finite variance and independent, identically distributed outputs justify the usual central-limit approximation as $R$ grows. They do not guarantee a good interval at a particular finite $R$, especially with rare or heavily weighted events. Four times as many replications halves the theoretical MCSE; it does not force every realized error to halve. To target theoretical MCSE $\epsilon$, a known Bernoulli probability would require $R\ge p_H(1-p_H)/\epsilon^2$; pilot estimates need care when events are scarce.

Zero observed events can produce false precision.

At $R=100$, the chance of no simulated 30-day low period is $(1-p_{30})^{100}\approx0.4554$. The estimate and sample MCSE would both be zero. A normal interval $[0,0]$ is uninformative about this positive probability. For a fixed-size independent Bernoulli sample with zero events, a one-sided 95% exact upper bound is $1-0.05^{1/R}$—about 0.0295 at $R=100$.

Here, a replication is one independent first-hit simulation. Do not substitute the days of one dependent Markov path, or overlapping observed windows, into an iid standard-error formula. Fix the simulation budget before comparing results; repeatedly stopping when an ordinary interval looks satisfactory changes its coverage properties.

04 / Importance samplingSample a different model; keep the original target.

The original chain leaves low with probability $a\approx0.1493$ each day. A proposal with a smaller hazard $q=0.04$ produces long low periods more often. It is a computational sampling distribution, not a new fitted model or a real operational intervention.

Let $f$ be the target probability mass function and $g$ the proposal. For an event $A$, ordinary importance sampling uses

$$p=\mathbb E_f[\mathbf1_A(X)]=\mathbb E_g\left[\mathbf1_A(X)\frac{f(X)}{g(X)}\right],\qquad \widehat p_{\rm IS}=\frac1R\sum_{r=1}^R\mathbf1_A(X_r)w(X_r),\quad X_r\sim g.$$

The proposal must assign positive probability wherever the target contribution is nonzero. A finite second moment of the weighted contribution is needed for a finite variance and the usual MCSE/CLT argument.

For full stopped-path likelihood ratios we restrict $0<q<1$. If a first hit occurs at $t\le H$, the path contains $t-1$ low-to-low transitions followed by one low-to-high transition. If it is still low through $H$, all $H$ transitions stay low:

$$w(t)=\begin{cases}\left(\frac{1-a}{1-q}\right)^{t-1}\frac aq,&t\le H,\\[4pt]\left(\frac{1-a}{1-q}\right)^H,&t>H.\end{cases}$$

Only the no-hit paths contribute to this target. Write $r_H=(1-q)^H$ and $w_H=[(1-a)/(1-q)]^H$. Then $Z_r=w_H\mathbf1\{T_r^{(q)}>H\}$ and

$$\mathbb E_q[Z]=w_Hr_H=p_H,\qquad \operatorname{Var}_q(Z)=p_H^2\left(\frac1{r_H}-1\right),\qquad \widehat{\operatorname{MCSE}}_{\rm IS}=s_Z/\sqrt R.$$

At $H=30,q=0.04$, proposal tail probability is about 0.2939 and each nonzero contribution is about 0.02666. The estimator still averages to about 0.0078343. The theoretical crude-to-IS variance ratio is about 52.70; at $R=1,000$, exact MCSE falls from about 0.002788 to 0.000384.

The weights are part of the estimator.

A plain proposal event fraction estimates $r_H$, not $p_H$. Use the standard deviation of the weighted contributions for MCSE, not a Bernoulli formula applied to the final estimate. Do not divide by the observed sum of weights: self-normalized importance sampling is a different, generally biased finite-sample estimator. The target distribution here is already normalized.

# One independent proposal replication; time starts from low
Tq = rng.geometric(q)
log_weight_tail = H * (np.log1p(-a) - np.log1p(-q))
Z = float(Tq > H) * np.exp(log_weight_tail)
# Repeat R times: estimate = Z.mean(); MCSE = Z.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(R)

We use a geometric draw because the hazard remains constant before the first hit. This is equivalent to simulating the stopped two-state chain. It costs one random variate per replication; weights add arithmetic. The variance ratio is not a measured wall-clock speedup.

With $q=a$, weights are exactly 1 and the methods have the same theoretical variance; separate random streams need not give the same estimate. With $q=0.3,H=30,R=1,000$, the probability of seeing no nonzero weighted contribution is about 0.9777, while the exact MCSE is about 0.05218. A run reporting estimate 0 and sample MCSE 0 has missed the important tail.

Ordinary importance-sampling estimates of a probability can exceed 1 on some runs. Clipping them to $[0,1]$ would change the estimator and introduce bias. The proposal must be chosen for the target event; variance reduction is not automatic. Here log weights reduce avoidable numerical error, and the browser limits proposals to 0.001–0.999.

At $H=30$, there are 44 eligible low-state origins, Sep 5–Dec 1. Sixteen have no high day in their complete follow-up window: $16/44\approx0.3636$, versus fitted-model probability $0.0078343$. These overlapping windows are dependent and belong to a previously inspected period; they do not supply 44 independent Bernoulli observations or an untouched final test.

The large descriptive mismatch motivates checking seasonal transitions and richer state information. Smaller Monte Carlo error only makes the fitted model's prediction more precise. It does not establish the Markov assumption, quantify uncertainty in estimated transitions, identify latent unmet demand, or prove a causal benefit from changing bikes or staffing.

07 / Common random numbersPair the randomness when comparing scenarios.

Now ask a different question: how much does the seven-day first-hit probability change if the low-to-high hazard changes from $a_0=a$ to an assumed $a_1=1.25a$? This is a mathematical sensitivity scenario, not an estimated intervention effect. Let $p_j=1-(1-a_j)^7$ and $\Delta=p_1-p_0\approx0.08691$.

Generate one uniform $U_r$ for each pair and use it in both geometric inverse CDFs. A larger hazard produces a no-later first hit, so the indicators are nested. Replicate pairs remain independent of other pairs. With $D_r=I_{1r}-I_{0r}$:

$$\widehat\Delta=\frac1R\sum_rD_r,\qquad \widehat{\operatorname{MCSE}}(\widehat\Delta)=s_D/\sqrt R,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(D)=\operatorname{Var}(I_1)+\operatorname{Var}(I_0)-2\operatorname{Cov}(I_1,I_0).$$

Positive covariance reduces the variance of the difference. For this monotone example with $a_1\ge a_0$, $D$ is Bernoulli with probability $\Delta$, so $\operatorname{Var}(D)=\Delta(1-\Delta)$. Independent streams instead give $p_1(1-p_1)+p_0(1-p_0)$. The default variance ratio is about 5.02. Pairing can fail to help for other outputs or couplings; check the covariance.

08 / Verification & limitationsAudit the estimator, its uncertainty, and its target.

Mathematical checks

Verify $w_Hr_H=p_H$; sum full stopped-path proposal mass times weights to 1; set $q=a$; check analytical variance against repeated estimates. For paired comparisons, test identical scenarios and compute the sample variance of the differences.

Empirical checks

Preserve training dates, compare complete observed windows, and report model mismatch separately from MCSE. Vary the fitted-model assumptions only as labeled sensitivity analyses. Larger simulation budgets do not create more observed information.

The finite-sample standard errors here condition on the fitted transition matrix. They do not include parameter-estimation uncertainty or model misspecification. A parameter bootstrap for dependent data requires an appropriate resampling model; treating temporal observations as iid would not solve that problem.

This instructor-written flawed AI-style suggestion needs no account to audit:

# Claimed: unbiased probability estimate and its MCSE
Tq = rng.geometric(q, size=R)
weighted = (Tq > H) * weights
estimate = weighted.sum() / weights.sum()
mcse = np.sqrt(estimate * (1 - estimate) / R)
# Claimed: paired comparison uncertainty
paired_se = np.sqrt(scenario.var(ddof=1)/R + baseline.var(ddof=1)/R)

Identify the estimator changed by normalization, the missing weighted-output variance, and the omitted covariance. Also audit a proposal to fit transitions on all 365 days or stop whenever a running plot looks stable: each changes a different part of the protocol. A numerical answer inside $[0,1]$ is not evidence that the method is correct. Record consequential AI assistance and your own checks; “No AI used” is valid.
