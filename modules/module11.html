11 / Gaussian processes & prediction uncertainty

What should we
be uncertain about?

A smooth curve is only one part of a prediction. Use covariance kernels to express dependence, condition a Gaussian process on observations, and distinguish uncertainty about a latent function from variation in a new measurement.

45–55 min guided work25–35 min notebook practiceConcept Lab 6 · Part A

01 / Practical questionWhat happens beyond the observed inputs?

Suppose daily completed bike rentals vary around an unobserved demand curve. Nearby dates may carry information about one another, but the strength and range of that relationship must be modeled. A Gaussian process supplies a joint probability model for function values; it does not discover the right notion of similarity automatically.

Predict before running

Begin with five assigned synthetic observations to inspect the matrix calculation. Then use the same observed bike-sharing counts in a fixed-origin forecasting trial. The distinction between a mathematically valid posterior and a well-calibrated model remains essential.

By the end: construct a valid covariance matrix, simulate finite Gaussian vectors, compute a posterior with Cholesky solves, compare latent and new-observation intervals, explain mean reversion under a stationary kernel, and audit a chronological prediction comparison.

02 / Concepts & mathematicsA kernel specifies which values vary together.

A Gaussian process is a collection of random variables for which every finite vector is jointly Gaussian. Write $f\sim\operatorname{GP}(m,k)$. For inputs $X=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$,

$$f(X)\sim N(m(X),K),\qquad K_{ij}=k(x_i,x_j),\qquad m(x)=\mathbb E[f(x)].$$

Normal marginal distributions alone do not imply a jointly Gaussian process. A covariance kernel must be symmetric and positive semidefinite (PSD):

$$k(x,x')=k(x',x),\qquad a^TKa=\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\sum_i a_i f(x_i)\right)\geq0\quad\text{for every finite }X\text{ and vector }a.$$

Nonnegative entries are insufficient. The matrix $\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\2&1\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ has a negative eigenvalue, −1, and cannot be a covariance matrix. A valid kernel may also have negative covariances. Checking one finite matrix is an implementation check, not a proof of validity for every possible input set.

Three valid kernels with different dependence assumptions
KernelCovarianceInterpretation
Squared exponential / RBF$\sigma_f^2\exp[-(x-x')^2/(2\ell^2)]$Stationary, very smooth latent variation. Correlation at distance ℓ is $e^{-1/2}$.
Exponential$\sigma_f^2\exp[-|x-x'|/\ell]$Stationary, rougher latent variation. Correlation at distance ℓ is $e^{-1}$.
Brownian, x,x′ ≥ 0$\min(x,x')$Nonstationary variance x; reconnects to Module 10. Independent increments are a special property, not shared by every GP.

$\sigma_f$ is a marginal standard deviation, so its square appears in the kernel. The length scale $\ell>0$ has the same units as x. It controls how rapidly covariance falls, rather than defining a hard independence radius. The exponential and RBF length scales use different conventions and need not give the same correlation at the same numerical value.

RBF processes are infinitely mean-square differentiable; the exponential kernel has a cusp at zero and defines a process that is not mean-square differentiable. Its sample curves appear rougher. A finite plot is an illustration, not a differentiability test. Brownian covariance specifies Brownian finite-dimensional laws; a continuous version is also needed for the usual pathwise Brownian definition.

distance = np.abs(x[:, None] - x[None, :])
K = amplitude**2 * np.exp(-distance**2 / (2 * length**2))
L = np.linalg.cholesky(K + 1e-10 * np.eye(len(x)))
values = mean_vector + L @ rng.normal(size=len(x))

For positive definite K, $m+Lz$ with $LL^T=K$ and $z\sim N(0,I)$ has the desired Gaussian distribution. A PSD matrix can be singular; an eigenvalue factorization handles that case. Here a stated $10^{-10}$ diagonal jitter stabilizes Cholesky on fine grids. It is a tiny numerical perturbation, not measured observation variance or permission to repair an invalid kernel.

03 / Worked computationCondition the joint Gaussian distribution.

Assume $y_i=f(x_i)+\epsilon_i$, with mutually independent $\epsilon_i\sim N(0,\sigma_n^2)$, independent of f. This is a Gaussian observation model. Define $A=K_{XX}+\sigma_n^2I$, $K_{*X}=k(X_*,X)$ and $K_{**}=k(X_*,X_*)$. For invertible A,

$$\begin{aligned}m_*&=m(X_*)+K_{*X}A^{-1}[y-m(X)],\\ V_f&=K_{**}-K_{*X}A^{-1}K_{X*},\\ V_y&=V_f+\sigma_n^2I.\end{aligned}$$

$V_f$ describes the latent function at prediction inputs. $V_y$ describes new observations with fresh independent noise, even when a prediction input equals a training input. Adding the noise term twice, or using latent uncertainty to evaluate noisy outcomes, changes the target.

One-observation example: let $f(0)\sim N(0,1)$, observe $y(0)=0.5$, and assume noise SD 1. Then A=2, posterior latent mean at zero is 0.25, and latent variance is 0.5. A new observation at zero has variance 1.5. Even at an observed input, learning the latent value and predicting a fresh noisy measurement are different tasks.

Use the inverse notation to understand the formula, but compute by solving triangular systems. With $A+\delta I=LL^T$ and numerical jitter $\delta=10^{-10}$:

L = np.linalg.cholesky(K + (noise_sd**2 + jitter) * np.eye(n))
alpha = solve_triangular(L.T,
    solve_triangular(L, y - prior_mean, lower=True), lower=False)
V = solve_triangular(L, Kxs, lower=True)
posterior_mean = prior_mean_star + Kxs.T @ alpha
latent_cov = Kss - V.T @ V
observation_var = np.diag(latent_cov) + noise_sd**2

Jitter is not added again to the new-observation variance. Tiny roundoff errors may be treated within a documented numerical tolerance; a substantial negative variance is a failed calculation to investigate. Do not silently clip predicted counts or widen the diagonal until a bad model appears valid.

$$m_*(x)\ \pm\ 1.2815516\sqrt{V_f(x,x)}\quad\text{or}\quad m_*(x)\ \pm\ 1.2815516\sqrt{V_y(x,x)}.$$

These are pointwise central 80% conditional intervals. They are not an 80% band for the entire path, nor a guarantee that 80% of any small evaluation set will be covered. With fixed kernel, noise, and input locations, the posterior covariance does not depend on the observed y values; the mean does. Fitting hyperparameters to y can create indirect dependence.

Gaussian conditioning follows the supplied Section 14 notes. Observation-noise and computational reference: Rasmussen & Williams (2006), Gaussian Processes for Machine Learning, Chapter 2, equations 2.22–2.24 and Algorithm 2.1.

04 / Interactive experimentChange dependence, then inspect both uncertainties.

Use x=[0, 0.8, 1.8, 3, 4.2] and y=[0.2, 1, −0.4, 0.6, −0.2]. These are assigned teaching values, not real measurements or a claim about an underlying true curve. The prior mean is zero and amplitude is one. A grid of 61 points from 0 to 6 includes extrapolation past the final observation.

Predict: increase ℓ from 0.3 to 2. Will the posterior mean stay near the observations farther into the gap? Then increase noise SD: should the fitted curve interpolate less closely?

Changing the seed changes illustrative draws and empirical covariance, but not the analytic posterior mean or variance. The 4,000-vector covariance check uses independent vectors, not the correlated coordinates of one curve as iid replicates. Lines connect sampled grid values; they do not generate the unobserved process between those points.

Try the limits: noise SD zero produces interpolation up to the tiny jitter perturbation. Positive noise leaves latent uncertainty and permits smoothing. Far from all training inputs, RBF and exponential cross-covariances approach zero: the mean returns to the specified prior mean, latent variance approaches $\sigma_f^2$, and new-observation variance approaches $\sigma_f^2+\sigma_n^2$. A longer length scale delays this return; it does not create a justified trend forecast.

A revealing failure case: with the RBF kernel, ℓ=2, and noise SD zero, the assigned observations are interpolated, yet the mean at x=6 is about −9.004. A long-scale, noiseless assumption can produce extreme finite-distance extrapolation before the mean eventually returns to zero farther away. This is a consequence of the assumed model, not evidence that a successful matrix solve guarantees sensible predictions.

Why a narrow conditional band can still be misleading

The conditional covariance formula knows the chosen kernel, input geometry, and noise level. It does not automatically know that the kernel is wrong, a trend has changed, or its fitted parameters are uncertain. A confident prediction can therefore be miscalibrated. A good numerical covariance check verifies an implementation, while coverage, residuals, and decision performance test the model against observations.

05 / Real-data applicationIssue every prediction before September begins.

The unchanged UCI sample contains 365 observed 2011 daily system-wide completed-rental counts. January–August supplies 243 training days; September–December supplies 122 later targets. Use day index x=0,…,364 and fit the training mean $\bar C_{\mathrm{tr}}$ and population SD $s_{\mathrm{tr}}$:

$$z_t=\frac{C_t-\bar C_{\mathrm{tr}}}{s_{\mathrm{tr}}},\qquad \bar C_{\mathrm{tr}}=\frac1{243}\sum_{t=0}^{242} C_t,\qquad s_{\mathrm{tr}}^2=\frac1{243}\sum_{t=0}^{242}(C_t-\bar C_{\mathrm{tr}})^2.$$

For standardized counts, set prior mean zero, amplitude 1, and observation noise SD 0.5. Compare two prespecified sensitivity scenarios, RBF length scales 14 and 45 days. They are instructional assumptions, not measured truths or settings optimized on September–December. On the original count scale, the prior mean is the training mean and amplitude is the fitted training SD.

Forecast protocol: condition only on data through August 31, freeze the entire fitted model, and predict every September–December day from that single origin. Do not feed later observed counts back into this forecast. Compare with two equally frozen baselines: the training mean and the last training count, 5058. All scores use the same 122 targets.

Module 10 used rolling one-day forecasts with the previous observed count available at each origin. This module uses horizons 1–122 days from one fixed origin. Their scores answer different questions and cannot establish that one model family is superior without matching the evaluation protocol.

The Gaussian observation law is a continuous approximation to integer counts, and can assign probability to negative values. Predictions and bounds are not clipped or rounded to disguise this. The iid noise term stands for unmodeled residual variation, not a measured sensor-error variance. Calendar effects, weather, changing behavior, residual dependence, and count constraints are omitted.

06 / Validation & interpretationWider uncertainty is not the same as better calibration.

Latent SDNew-observation SDDashed: corresponding prior limits

These stationary-kernel uncertainties tend toward finite prior limits far from the data. They need not grow without bound with the forecast horizon. They also exclude uncertainty in the fitted center/scale, kernel choice, length scale, and noise level. The latent curve is unobserved, so observed counts cannot directly validate a claimed latent-curve coverage rate.

Implementation checks

Check symmetry and PSD, reconstruct $LL^T$, compare a one-observation posterior with hand calculation, verify $V_y-V_f=\sigma_n^2I$, and perturb y while holding inputs and parameters fixed. Use seed replay for simulated draws.

Model checks

Compare identical forecast targets against simple baselines. Report coverage together with interval width, inspect errors over time and training residual dependence, and state which inputs were available at the origin. A wider interval can raise coverage without improving predictions.

The two observed coverage fractions are about 92.6% and 90.2%, both above the nominal 80%. This suggests conservative intervals on this aggregate development comparison, but does not certify conditional calibration, independence, or coverage at every horizon. These temporally related outcomes are not iid Bernoulli trials for an automatic binomial error bar.

Inspect the notebook's training residual plot without presenting in-sample fit as predictive success. Optional extension · a richer kernel or count observation law

A sum of valid PSD kernels is valid: add a weekly periodic component to represent an explicit calendar hypothesis, and choose its parameters using earlier data only. Alternatively, give counts a Poisson likelihood with a latent log-intensity GP. The latter loses this simple conjugate Gaussian update and needs a separate approximation and validation.
