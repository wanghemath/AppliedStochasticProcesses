What can a year of bike-rental counts tell us about future high-demand days?

40–50 min guided work · 25–35 min notebook practicePrerequisites: basic probability & PythonObserved data + synthetic experiments

Learning targets: distinguish a process from a path; define a measurable outcome; estimate a simple model; verify a simulation; identify evidence against its assumptions. This is the first half of Lab 1, completed with Markov chains in Week 2.

01 / Practical questionHow often might a planning threshold be exceeded?

Imagine a bike-share planning team flags days with more than 4,000 completed rentals for additional operational attention. We observe a sequence of daily counts and want to estimate how many flagged days a future 30-day period might contain.

Separate the measurement from the scenario

The rental counts are observed. The threshold of 4,000 rentals/day and the future 30-day window are teaching scenario assumptions. The threshold is not a measured fleet capacity, and completed rentals do not reveal unmet demand, simultaneous bike availability, or queueing delays.

Our first model deliberately keeps only one bit per day: above the threshold or not. That makes the model easy to check—and its omissions easy to see.

02 / Concepts & mathematicsA process is a collection of random variables.

A random variable assigns a numerical value to a random outcome. Formally, it is a measurable function on a probability space $(\Omega,\mathcal F,\mathbb P)$. A stochastic process is a family $\{X_t:t\in T\}$ of random variables on that same space. Here $T=\{1,\ldots,365\}$ indexes the observed days (the model can extend to future days) and $X_t$ is the daily rental count, with state space $\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$.

Fix a day $t$: $X_t$ is a random variable. Fix an outcome $\omega$: the sequence $t\mapsto X_t(\omega)$ is a sample path. The 2011 dataset is one observed path, not 365 independent replications of the whole year. A process model specifies how values behave jointly across time.

Turn counts into a binary outcome using a threshold $c$:

$$Y_t=\mathbf{1}\{X_t>c\},\qquad Y_t\in\{0,1\}.$$

Our baseline assumes independent, identically distributed days:

$$Y_t\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p),\quad \mathbb P(Y_t=1)=p,\quad\mathbb P(Y_t=0)=1-p.$$

“Identically distributed” means the same probability $p$ each day. “Independent” means knowing other days does not change a day's probability. In particular, for $0<p<1$, $\mathbb P(Y_{t+1}=1\mid Y_t=1)=p$. Constant marginal probabilities alone do not imply independence.

$$\mathbb E[Y_t]=p,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y_t)=p(1-p).$$

For the number of flagged days in an $n$-day period, $S_n=\sum_{t=1}^{n}Y_t$:

$$S_n\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n,p),\qquad \mathbb E[S_n]=np,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(S_n)=np(1-p).$$

The binomial distribution and this variance require the iid assumption. More generally,

$$\operatorname{Var}(S_n)=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\operatorname{Var}(Y_t)+2\sum_{s<t}\operatorname{Cov}(Y_s,Y_t).$$

Positive dependence can make totals more variable than the independent-day model suggests. Later modules will model that dependence explicitly. Expectation still adds without independence, so matching a mean is a weak check.

Probability refresher: a distribution is more than its mean

Probabilities must be nonnegative and sum to one. For $k=0,\ldots,n$, the binomial probability is

$$\mathbb P(S_n=k)=\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k}.$$

At $p=0$ the total is always zero; at $p=1$ it is always $n$. At interior values, many different totals are possible. A forecast should communicate this variability, not only the expected value.

03 / Worked computationSimulate a model we can verify.

Start with a synthetic demonstration: $p=0.45$, $n=30$ days and $R=1{,}000$ independent simulated paths. Before generating anything, the model gives $\mathbb E[S_{30}]=13.5$ and $\operatorname{Var}(S_{30})=7.425$.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(6231)
p, n, R = 0.45, 30, 1000
paths = rng.binomial(1, p, size=(R, n))
totals = paths.sum(axis=1)
print(totals.mean(), totals.var(ddof=1))

Each row is one possible 30-day path. The simulated mean and variance should be near the formulas, not equal to them. For independent replications, the Monte Carlo standard error of the simulated mean is

$$\operatorname{MCSE}(\overline S_n)=\sqrt{\frac{np(1-p)}{R}}.$$

Increasing $R$ reduces numerical simulation error. It does not supply new observations, remove seasonality, or make the model's assumptions true. A fixed seed makes a computation reproducible; changing it shows the expected Monte Carlo variability.

05 / Real-data applicationEstimate from the past. Check on later days.

We use the 365 daily observations from 2011 in the UCI Bike Sharing dataset. Each row records a date and a count of completed rentals in the Capital Bikeshare system. The bundled subset preserves the source values and time order.

  1. Audit: parse dates, check daily continuity, unique rows, missing values, and nonnegative integer counts.
  2. Fit: use January 1–August 31 only, giving $m=243$ training days.
  3. Validate: keep September 1–December 31 as $122$ later days. Do not shuffle temporal observations.
$$\widehat p=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{t\in\mathrm{train}}\mathbf{1}\{X_t>c\}.$$

At $c=4000$, $\widehat p=109/243\approx0.4486$. Plug this estimate into the iid model. A future 30-day total then has conditional model mean $30\widehat p\approx13.46$.

This plug-in calculation treats $\widehat p$ as fixed. The simulation alone does not account for estimation uncertainty, changing demand, or omitted covariates. The notebook distinguishes these sources of uncertainty.

06 / Validation & interpretationCheck the implementation and the model separately.

Does the code simulate its stated model?

Compare simulated means and variances with the binomial formulas. Check that paths contain only 0 and 1 before summing. Test the deterministic endpoints $p=0$ and $p=1$.

Agreement supports the implementation. It does not validate the assumptions against rental data.

Does the model describe relevant data behavior?

Compare held-out rates, temporal clustering, and daily patterns. Ask whether the model is useful for the planning question, and what it cannot predict.

Changing seed cannot repair an inappropriate independence or constant-probability assumption.

A switch occurs when a flagged day is followed by an unflagged day, or vice versa. For $h$ iid Bernoulli observations with fixed $p$,

$$K=\sum_{t=2}^{h}\mathbf{1}\{Y_t\ne Y_{t-1}\},\qquad \mathbb E[K]=2(h-1)p(1-p).$$

With $c=4000$, the held-out data contain 27 switches; the fitted iid model expects about 59.86. The test flagged-day fraction is $48/122\approx0.3934$, compared with $\widehat p\approx0.4486$. The fraction alone hides much of the clustering. These descriptive discrepancies motivate richer models; they do not by themselves identify the physical mechanism.

The notebook also computes the Brier score, the average squared error of a probability forecast, $h^{-1}\sum_t(\widehat p-Y_t)^2$. Smaller is better on the same held-out outcomes. Compare with an always-unflagged baseline. Neither a favorable score nor a close mean establishes independence.

Limits of the decision claim

The iid model omits seasonality, weather, trend, and dependence. Daily system-wide completed rentals cannot identify service times, station bottlenecks, or lost customers. Comparing threshold scenarios is a descriptive planning exercise; it does not estimate the causal effect of adding bikes or staff.

Three uncertainties: future path variability remains even with known $p$; parameter uncertainty comes from estimating $p$ using finite observations; model uncertainty concerns whether iid Bernoulli days are appropriate at all. Monte Carlo error is a separate computational issue.

08 / Practice & extensionMake a small, defensible claim.

Complete these four prompts in the notebook. Allow about 25–35 minutes for brief responses: a small computation or plot and 2–4 interpretive sentences each. Guided notebook work can be completed in class; this is part of Lab 1, not another project.

  1. Identify the object. State the time index and state space for $Y_t$ and $S_n$. Distinguish one observed path, 1,000 simulated paths, and 1,000 independently observed years. Is $\widehat p$ a possible state? What extra information beyond marginal probabilities defines a process?
  2. Monte Carlo precision. Predict the change in MCSE from $R=250$ to $R=4000$ at $n=30$ and $p=\widehat p$. Use seeds 6231 and 6232 at both replication counts. Compare the mean, exact mean, process SD, and MCSE. Explain why a larger run need not have a smaller absolute error.
  3. A held-out check with units. Divide the first 120 test days into four nonoverlapping 30-day blocks. Explain why two days are unused. Compare block totals with the fitted binomial distribution. Explain the limits of four blocks, why shuffling changes the question, and one additional measured variable that could improve the model.
