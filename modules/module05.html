05 / MCMC & Bayesian calibration

Is the sampler exploring the posterior?

A transition probability is uncertain, and a simulated parameter chain can explore that uncertainty badly. Use an exact benchmark to check Metropolis–Hastings before trusting its output.

45–55 min guided work25–35 min notebook practiceConcept Lab 3 · Part A

01 / Practical questionThe data chain and the sampling chain have different jobs.

Keep the observed bike-rental state $Y_t=\mathbf1\{X_t>4000\}$. Previously we used the fixed estimate $\widehat a=20/134$ for the chance that a low day is followed by a high day. Now we model uncertainty about $a=P_{01}$ using a posterior distribution and propagate it into a first-passage prediction.

Observed process

$Y_t\in\{0,1\}$ records rental states. Its time step is one calendar day. The fitted transition matrix describes a proposed model of those observations.

Computational process

$a^{(m)}\in(0,1)$ is an MCMC state. Its index counts algorithm iterations. We construct its transition rule to have the posterior density as an invariant distribution.

A posterior density for $a$ is not the stationary distribution of low and high rental states from Module 3. The algorithm's proposals and acceptance rate do not describe bike-rental transitions.

Predict before running

By the end: derive a simple posterior, implement a correct MH step, retain rejected states, diagnose mixing with several complementary checks, and distinguish posterior uncertainty from the computational error of an estimated posterior mean.

02 / Bayesian calibrationStart with a posterior we can check exactly.

Use only Jan 1–Aug 31: 243 days and 242 transitions wholly within training. The observed counts remain $N=\begin{pmatrix}114&20\\19&89\end{pmatrix}$. Define $a=P_{01}$ and $b=P_{10}$. Conditional on the first observed state, the homogeneous first-order Markov model gives

$$L(a,b\mid Y_0,\ldots,Y_{242})\propto a^{20}(1-a)^{114}\,b^{19}(1-b)^{89}.$$

This is a likelihood for a dependent path: multiply the conditional transition probabilities. The factorization does not assume that observed days or overlapping transition pairs are independent. Conditioning on the initial state matters; imposing a parameter-dependent stationary initial law would change the likelihood.

Choose independent $\operatorname{Beta}(1,1)$ priors for $a$ and $b$. A Beta prior with shapes $A_0,B_0$ has density proportional to $a^{A_0-1}(1-a)^{B_0-1}$ on $(0,1)$. Bayes' rule adds the relevant transition counts:

$$\pi(a\mid Y)\propto L(a\mid Y)\pi_0(a),\qquad a\mid Y\sim\operatorname{Beta}(21,115),\qquad b\mid Y\sim\operatorname{Beta}(20,90).$$

Posterior independence here follows from the factorized path likelihood and independent priors, not from independence of the observations. The uniform prior is a visible modeling choice on the probability scale; it is not automatically neutral under another parameterization.

$$\mathbb E[a\mid Y]=\frac A{A+B},\qquad \operatorname{Var}(a\mid Y)=\frac{AB}{(A+B)^2(A+B+1)},\quad (A,B)=(21,115).$$

A 95% Bayesian credible interval contains 95% of the posterior mass under the stated assumptions. It is not an interval for a future binary state, and it is not a Monte Carlo error bar. Here we can use exact Beta formulas and independent Beta draws directly. MCMC is deliberately used as a verified teaching example; it is more useful when direct posterior calculation or sampling is unavailable.

03 / Metropolis–HastingsPropose, compare, and store one state every time.

For a target density $\pi(\theta)$ known up to a normalizing constant, propose $\theta'\sim q(\cdot\mid\theta)$. Accept with probability

$$\alpha(\theta,\theta')=\min\left\{1,\frac{\pi(\theta')q(\theta\mid\theta')}{\pi(\theta)q(\theta'\mid\theta)}\right\}.$$

Draw $U\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(0,1)$. If $U<\alpha$, move to the proposal; otherwise remain at the current state. Store the resulting state after every proposal. Removing rejections produces a different, generally biased sample from the chain's jump locations.

For this lesson use an untruncated Gaussian random walk $a'=a+\sigma Z$, $Z\sim N(0,1)$. Its proposal density is symmetric. With unnormalized log posterior $\ell(a)=20\log a+114\log(1-a)$,

$$\log\alpha=\min\{0,\ell(a')-\ell(a)\}.$$

Set $\ell(a')=-\infty$ outside $(0,1)$. The unknown posterior normalizing constant cancels.

Reject a proposal outside the support.

Do not clip a negative proposal to zero or repeatedly draw until it is inside the interval. Those operations change the proposal kernel. A truncated-normal proposal generally needs its reverse/forward Hastings correction. The simple log-ratio formula here is valid because we draw once from the symmetric untruncated Gaussian and reject invalid proposals.

proposal = current + proposal_sd * rng.normal()
log_ratio = log_target(proposal) - log_target(current)
if np.log(rng.random()) < min(0.0, log_ratio):
    current = proposal
samples[iteration] = current  # always store, including a rejection

The MH construction satisfies detailed balance with the target: forward and reverse probability flows match. This makes the posterior invariant. Appropriate irreducibility and aperiodicity support eventual convergence; none of these statements guarantees that a particular finite run has mixed.

05 / Mixing diagnosticsStored draws are not independent information.

A trace should repeatedly explore the posterior's relevant range, and dispersed starts should agree after warm-up. Histograms can hide ordering and chain disagreement. Autocorrelation measures association between draws separated by a lag; slow decay indicates that nearby draws provide similar information.

A transparent, limited split-R-hat calculation

Split each retained chain in half. For $M$ resulting sequences of equal length $n$, let $\bar a_j$ and $s_j^2$ denote their means and variances, and let $\bar a$ be their mean of means:

$$W=\frac1M\sum_j s_j^2,\qquad B_{\rm between}=\frac{n}{M-1}\sum_j(\bar a_j-\bar a)^2,$$ $$\widehat V=\frac{n-1}{n}W+\frac1nB_{\rm between},\qquad \widehat R_{\rm basic}=\sqrt{\widehat V/W}.$$

Between-chain disagreement raises this statistic. A value near 1 is reassuring only in combination with other evidence; chains can share the same failure. Constant chains or constant split halves are flagged as invalid, not assigned a good score. Finite-sample values can lie slightly below 1.

Teaching diagnostics have limits.

This is basic unranked split R-hat, not modern rank-normalized and folded R-hat. Our ESS estimate is for a mean, not bulk or tail ESS. Production analyses should use validated diagnostics appropriate to the target, including rank-based R-hat and function-specific bulk/tail checks. No finite diagnostic proves convergence.

Batch means estimate computational error

Within each of $C=4$ retained chains, divide $n=Kb$ draws into $K$ nonoverlapping batches of length $b=50$. Let $\bar a_{jk}$ be a batch mean. If batches are long enough relative to dependence and the chain is approximately stationary,

$$\widehat\sigma^2_{\rm asym,j}=b\,s^2(\bar a_{j1},\ldots,\bar a_{jK}),\qquad \widehat{\operatorname{MCSE}}^2(\bar a_{\rm pooled})=\frac1{C^2}\sum_{j=1}^C\frac{\widehat\sigma^2_{\rm asym,j}}n.$$

Here $n=3,000$ retained draws per chain gives 60 batches by default. A rough mean-specific effective sample size is

$$\widehat{\operatorname{ESS}}_{\rm mean}=\frac{C^{-1}\sum_j s_j^2}{\widehat{\operatorname{MCSE}}^2(\bar a_{\rm pooled})},$$

using the average within-chain variance of retained draws in the numerator.

This expresses equivalent iid information for estimating this mean, assuming the diagnostics are applicable. Batch choices affect the estimate; an estimated ESS may exceed the stored count from sampling variability or negative dependence, so we do not silently cap it. Too-short batches can miss long dependence. A small calculated MCSE from chains that have not explored the target is unreliable.

The browser warns when basic split R-hat exceeds 1.01, mean ESS is below 400, or a statistic is undefined. These are teaching screens, not sufficient convergence conditions. The notebook varies batch size. Routine thinning discards useful draws and is not a cure for poor mixing; improve the run or sampler and retain the states unless storage constraints require a justified alternative.

Keep the units clear: posterior SD describes uncertainty about $a$ under the model. MCSE describes numerical uncertainty in our estimate of the posterior mean. More MCMC iterations can reduce MCSE; they do not create new rental observations or automatically shrink the posterior SD.

06 / Posterior prediction & dataAverage the prediction over parameter uncertainty.

Starting from a low day, conditional on one fixed parameter $a$, the no-high probability through $H$ days is $p_H(a)=(1-a)^H$. A posterior predictive probability integrates this function:

$$\Pr(T>H\mid Y)=\mathbb E[(1-a)^H\mid Y]=\frac{\mathrm B(A,B+H)}{\mathrm B(A,B)}=\prod_{j=0}^{H-1}\frac{B+j}{A+B+j}.$$

For a simulated future path, draw one $a$ from its posterior and hold it fixed for all $H$ days. Redrawing the parameter every day defines another model. Because this prediction is nonlinear, integrating it differs from substituting the posterior mean.

At $H=30$, the integrated probability is about 0.010824, versus 0.006528 at the posterior mean and 0.007834 at the earlier maximum-likelihood estimate. MCMC approximates the integral by averaging $(1-a^{(m)})^H$. Its MCSE must be recomputed for that transformed sequence; the MCSE of the parameter mean is not interchangeable.

The equal-tail 95% posterior interval for $p_{30}(a)$ is about $[0.000591,0.043907]$. It represents uncertainty about a model probability. It is not a predictive interval for a future binary event or for a fraction computed from 44 dependent windows.

The 44 eligible origins run Sep 5–Dec 1; 16 have no high day, giving $16/44\approx0.3636$. The large descriptive discrepancy remains after including parameter uncertainty. Bayesian calibration conditions on the model; it does not repair seasonal transition changes, omitted states, or other misspecification.

07 / Verification & interpretationCheck the sampler and the modeling assumptions separately.

Sampler verification

Check support, the log acceptance ratio, and reverse/forward proposal densities. Retain repeated states. Compare sampled moments and quantiles with the exact Beta law. Test several starts, proposal scales, and batch lengths. Use the exact predictive integral to check transformed draws.

Model assessment

Document the prior, conditional path likelihood, fitting boundary, and observation units. Inspect later data with a stated prediction target. A correct MH implementation or a near-one diagnostic does not establish model adequacy.

This instructor-written flawed AI-style suggestion can be audited without any AI account:

proposal = np.clip(current + scale * rng.normal(), 0.001, 0.999)
if rng.random() < min(1, target(proposal) / target(current)):
    current = proposal
    samples.append(current)      # only accepted states
se = np.std(samples, ddof=1) / np.sqrt(len(samples))
# Claim: high acceptance and this small SE prove convergence.

Explain why clipping changes the proposal, why omitting rejections changes the sampled distribution, and why an iid SE ignores dependence. High acceptance can reflect tiny moves. Repair the algorithm and uncertainty estimate, then test it against the conjugate benchmark. Record consequential AI help and your own checks; “No AI used” is valid.
