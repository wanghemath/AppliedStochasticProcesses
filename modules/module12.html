01 / Practical questionWhat would count as an improvement?

A model can lower squared error while increasing absolute error. Its mean predictions can improve while its intervals become too narrow. A beautifully fitted historical curve can also use information that was unavailable when a forecast should have been issued. None of these questions is settled by a single attractive plot.

Predict before running

This module synthesizes the modeling and uncertainty work from earlier modules. We use the same observed bike counts and a small, stated set of forecasting rules. No new model family or additional project is required.

By the end: define a chronological evaluation protocol, select a GP setting using an earlier period, compare point and distribution forecasts on identical later dates, diagnose leakage, vary a predictive uncertainty assumption, and interpret a paired block-bootstrap comparison cautiously.

02 / Freeze the protocolA forecast may only use what was already known.

Let $C_t$ be observed completed rentals on day t, indexed from January 1 as t=0. A one-day forecast for t must be a function of the information available through t−1, denoted $\mathcal F_{t-1}$. Date features known in advance may be used; the realized count $C_t$ may not.

The two baselines also need a stated predictive distribution before they can receive probability scores. Assume a zero-mean Gaussian error about the lag forecast, and estimate its SD from 60 earlier baseline errors:

$$\widehat C_t=C_{t-q},\qquad s_{t,q}^2=\frac1{60}\sum_{j=t-60}^{t-1}(C_j-C_{j-q})^2,\qquad q\in\{1,7\}.$$

This is the mean squared residual around an assumed zero error mean, not a centered sample variance. Lag seven needs up to 67 past days; that information is available to every method at the origin. A common information boundary does not require identical modeling windows. Zero residual scale would require a separately specified degenerate model or a justified variance rule; it is not silently replaced with an arbitrary floor.

Different protocol from Module 11: that module issued all later forecasts at August 31 without updates. Here each earlier observed outcome may enter subsequent origins. The old and new scores cannot establish a fair model ranking unless origin, horizon, and information are matched.

Chronological selection is an inner evaluation step. Selecting repeatedly on the assessment period turns it into another tuning set. Randomly shuffled temporal folds can expose future outcomes or future-fitted preprocessing when the deployment question is forecasting.

Temporal evaluation reference: Hyndman & Athanasopoulos, Forecasting: Principles and Practice, time-series cross-validation. The course uses an earlier selection period and rolling origins rather than claiming that a random split reproduces future deployment.

03 / Choose the scorePoint accuracy, probability accuracy, and coverage answer different questions.

For N matched predictions with errors $e_t=C_t-\mu_t$, define

$$\operatorname{MAE}=\frac1N\sum_t|e_t|,\qquad \operatorname{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac1N\sum_t e_t^2}.$$

Absolute loss targets a conditional median; squared loss targets a conditional mean. A Gaussian forecast uses the same value for both, but occasional large errors receive more weight in RMSE. State the loss that reflects the actual decision before looking for a winner.

For a continuous Gaussian predictive approximation $N(\mu_t,s_t^2)$ with $s_t>0$, the negative log density is

$$\operatorname{NLD}_t=\tfrac12\log(2\pi)+\log s_t+\frac{(C_t-\mu_t)^2}{2s_t^2}.$$

It penalizes both unnecessarily diffuse forecasts and errors made with excessive confidence. It is a density score in the stated rentals/day units, not a probability mass for an integer count. Do not mix its numerical values with count-PMF log scores or scores computed in different units. Gaussian observations are a continuous count approximation and may assign positive probability to negative counts; predictions and intervals are not clipped.

For the stated event $B_t=\mathbf1\{C_t\geq4500\}$, use its predicted probability, not a density evaluated at the threshold:

$$p_t=1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{4500-\mu_t}{s_t}\right),\qquad \operatorname{Brier}=\frac1N\sum_t(p_t-B_t)^2.$$

Lower negative log density and Brier scores are better. Proper scoring rules reward reporting the predictive distribution honestly in expectation under their applicable outcome model; they do not guarantee a finite-sample ordering. Brier evaluates this particular binary event, so changing the event changes the question.

For a central $1-\alpha=80\%$ prediction interval $[l_t,u_t]$, record coverage and mean width together. The interval score adds penalties for missed outcomes:

$$\operatorname{IS}_{\alpha}(l,u;C)=(u-l)+\frac2\alpha(l-C)\mathbf1\{C<l\}+\frac2\alpha(C-u)\mathbf1\{C>u\},\qquad \alpha=0.2.$$

Lower interval score is better. Coverage alone can be improved by making every interval enormous. A central 80% interval is pointwise, not a band that covers the whole time series with probability 80%.

Worked score check: if $Y\sim N(0,1)$ and the realized value is 0, negative log density is $\tfrac12\log(2\pi)\approx0.91894$. The central 80% interval is [−1.28155,1.28155]; it covers the observation, so its interval score equals its width, about 2.56310. For the example event Y≥1, the probability is about 0.15866 and its Brier contribution is about 0.02517 because the event did not occur.

Proper scoring reference: Gneiting & Raftery (2007), Strictly Proper Scoring Rules, Prediction, and Estimation, including the central interval score in Section 6.2. Scores here are oriented as losses: lower is better.

05 / Sensitivity analysisVary an assumption without silently changing the target.

Keep the selected GP's means fixed and multiply its new-observation predictive SDs by a factor c. This directly tests whether the conclusions depend on the reported uncertainty scale. It is a diagnostic alteration of the predictive distribution, not a GP refit.

$$N(\mu_t,s_t^2)\ \longrightarrow\ N(\mu_t,c^2s_t^2),\qquad c\in\{0.5,1,2\}.$$

Predict: can changing c affect MAE or RMSE? Can it change the Brier score even though every predictive mean stays fixed?

In this comparison, multiplying SD by 2 increases coverage from 70 to 101 of 122 and improves negative log density. This suggests that the original predictive distribution is too concentrated for many later outcomes, while leaving point errors untouched. It does not identify why the model is wrong or establish that doubling SD will generalize. Selecting c after seeing these outcomes requires a new evaluation before a final performance claim.

For the sustained project, vary a few assumptions that can affect its decision: a forecast horizon, kernel range, rate estimate, or cost ratio. Define the range and decision criterion in advance. Report whether the recommendation changes, as well as which unknown inputs need better measurement. A sensitivity result is not a causal effect of changing the real system.

06 / Failure caseA lower error can come from seeing the answer.

Start with honest persistence $\widehat C_t=C_{t-1}$. Deliberately contaminate it with the target:

$$\widetilde C_t=(1-w)\widehat C_t+wC_t,\qquad 0\leq w\leq1.$$

For nonnegative weights in this range, MAE and RMSE are multiplied by 1−w. At w=1 the reported prediction is the observed answer. This is a labeled leakage demonstration applied to the real counts, not a fitted model or an achievable forecast.

Other leaks are less obvious: centering with the entire series, calculating residual scales using future errors, selecting a model on the outcomes later used to claim its performance, or using a centered moving average that includes the target. Perfect training fit is not evidence of a deployable forecast. Verify the data dependencies, not just the final array shape.

Prefix check: change an observation in the future and recompute. Predictions issued before observing that count must be unchanged, including the forecast for that count itself. Predictions issued afterward may legitimately change under rolling updates. The GP length-scale choice must not change when only September–December outcomes change.

07 / Uncertainty in comparisonPair the dates, then preserve local dependence.

Define a paired daily loss difference between the selected GP and persistence:

$$d_t=|C_t-\mu_t^{\mathrm{GP}}|-|C_t-C_{t-1}|,\qquad \bar d=\frac1{122}\sum_t d_t.$$

Positive differences favor persistence under absolute loss; negative differences favor the GP. Pairing preserves shared easy and difficult dates. Shuffling one model's errors before subtracting destroys that pairing. Here the observed mean difference is about +18.23 rentals/day, a small descriptive advantage for persistence on MAE.

Daily differences may be dependent. A moving-block bootstrap samples contiguous blocks from the observed difference sequence. For block length L, choose block starts uniformly from 0,…,N−L, draw $\lceil N/L\rceil$ blocks with replacement, concatenate, truncate to N, and compute a resampled mean. Repeat 2,000 times. The 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles give an approximate percentile interval.

starts = rng.integers(0, n - block_length + 1,
                      size=int(np.ceil(n / block_length)))
resample = np.concatenate([d[s:s+block_length] for s in starts])[:n]
resampled_mean = resample.mean()
# Repeat on the paired difference sequence, not on unpaired model losses.

L=1 reduces to iid resampling and ignores temporal dependence. Longer blocks retain more local structure but supply fewer effectively distinct blocks. No single choice is universally correct. The noncircular construction gives edge observations different resampling weights, so the bootstrap mean can differ from $\bar d$; this is especially visible with L=14 in this sample.

This approximation relies on sufficiently stable loss behavior and suitable weak dependence. It does not repair seasonality, distribution shift, or a protocol selected after seeing assessment outcomes. It conditions on the earlier GP choice and does not propagate model-selection uncertainty. The interval is neither a causal effect nor a guarantee of future superiority. Seed changes only the Monte Carlo resampling approximation; more resamples do not create new observed days.

Dependence and block-resampling reference: Shalizi, Simulation for Inference I — The Bootstrap. The notebook compares L=1, 7, and 14 and retains the same paired sign convention.
