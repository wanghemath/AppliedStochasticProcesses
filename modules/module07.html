07 / Poisson processes

Same total,
different timing.

An event count tells you how much activity occurred. An event process also describes when it occurred. Learn what changes when the rate varies—and what daily totals leave unidentified.

01 / Practical questionCan equal totals hide very different service pressure?

Imagine requests arriving at a service over four hours. One scenario has a steady rate; another concentrates arrivals in the middle; a third has a late peak. Each expects 24 requests overall. Would they need the same staffing schedule? Would the chance of seeing no requests in the next half-hour be the same?

Predict before running

The event paths below are synthetic demonstrations, with rates in events per hour. The later bike-sharing application uses observed completed rentals per calendar day. We do not reconstruct unobserved rental timestamps or claim to have measured rush-hour demand.

02 / Model & mathematicsCount events in calendar time.

Let $N(t)$ count events in $(0,t]$, with $N(0)=0$. A counting path is integer-valued and nondecreasing, with jumps of one when events occur. Unlike Module 6's population $Z_n$, $N(t)$ accumulates events; its events do not have to reproduce, and its count cannot decline.

A homogeneous Poisson process (HPP) with fixed rate $\lambda>0$ has independent increments over disjoint intervals and stationary increments: the distribution of a count depends only on the interval's length. For any $t,h\geq0$,

$$N(t+h)-N(t)\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda h),\qquad \Pr(N(t+h)-N(t)=k)=e^{-\lambda h}\frac{(\lambda h)^k}{k!}.$$

Its increment mean and variance both equal $\lambda h$. The rate has units events/time; the Poisson mean $\lambda h$ is an expected event count. A Poisson distribution for a single total does not, by itself, establish independent increments for the entire process.

Successive complete interarrival times $W_i$ in an HPP are iid exponential with rate $\lambda$:

$$\Pr(W_i>w)=e^{-\lambda w},\qquad \mathbb E[W_i]=\frac1\lambda,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(W_i)=\frac1{\lambda^2}.\quad(w\geq0)$$

The exponential law is memoryless: $\Pr(W>s+h\mid W>s)=e^{-\lambda h}$. At rate 6 events/hour the mean gap is $1/6$ hour, or ten minutes. NumPy's exponential sampler takes a scale, so use rng.exponential(1 / rate). Passing the rate as the scale reverses the relationship.

# Independent HPP checks: counts and complete gaps.
rng = np.random.default_rng(6231)
counts = rng.poisson(6 * 4, size=2000)
gaps = rng.exponential(scale=1 / 6, size=20000)
# Check count mean ≈ variance ≈ 24; gap mean ≈ 1/6.

Observation windows matter. The waiting-time check draws complete gaps independently. Keeping only gaps that finish before hour 4 preferentially keeps short gaps; the unfinished final interval is right-censored. Those retained gaps are not an uncensored iid sample from the exponential law.

03 / Time-varying ratesIntegrate the rate over the interval.

A nonhomogeneous Poisson process (NHPP) uses a deterministic, nonnegative, locally integrable intensity $\lambda(t)$. Define the cumulative intensity $\Lambda(t)=\int_0^t\lambda(u)\,du$. Disjoint increments remain independent, but their laws need not be stationary:

$$N(b)-N(a)\sim\operatorname{Poisson}\!\left(\Lambda(b)-\Lambda(a)\right),\qquad \mathbb E[N(t)]=\operatorname{Var}(N(t))=\Lambda(t).$$

A time-varying deterministic rate differs from a random rate or a rate that responds to past events. Mixing over a shared uncertain rate can induce dependence and extra variance; independent-increment claims apply here conditional on the fixed rate function.

Exact simulation without tiny time steps

For each constant-rate interval $(b_j,b_{j+1}]$, independently draw its count with mean $\lambda_j(b_{j+1}-b_j)$. Conditional on that count, place the events uniformly within the interval, then sort all event times. This is exact for the stated piecewise-constant intensity; it does not approximate the process with Bernoulli trials on a fine grid.

times = []
for a, b, rate in zip(breaks[:-1], breaks[1:], rates):
    count = rng.poisson(rate * (b - a))
    times.extend(rng.uniform(a, b, size=count))
times = np.sort(times)

More generally, given $N(T)=k$, the unordered event times are iid with density $\lambda(t)/\Lambda(T)$ on $(0,T]$, then sorted. Whole-window uniform times are correct only for a constant rate. Fixing the total at 24 changes the task: bin counts become multinomial and dependent, rather than independent Poisson counts. An unconditional simulator must retain a random Poisson total, either by summing independent Poisson bin counts or by drawing a total before conditional event locations.

The separate homogeneous waiting-time check always uses the average rate and is unchanged by profile choice. It does not describe the variable-rate process's raw gaps.

$$\widehat{\operatorname{MCSE}}(\overline{N(4)})=\frac{s_{N(4)}}{\sqrt R},\qquad \operatorname{MCSE}(\widehat p)=\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}R}.$$

Estimate the latter by substituting $\widehat p$ when $p$ is unknown. Use independent full paths as replications; points along one counting path are dependent. Here exact Poisson probabilities supply useful error benchmarks. More paths reduce numerical error, not real-world model error.

05 / A quiet intervalThe next wait depends on the calendar.

At a fixed time $s$, the NHPP probability of no event in the next $h$ hours is

$$\Pr\!\left(N(s+h)-N(s)=0\right)=\Pr(W_s>h)=\exp\!\left(-\int_s^{s+h}\lambda(u)\,du\right).$$

The rate at the start is insufficient when the interval crosses a rate change. For the default middle peak, $(0.8,1.3]$ has integrated rate $0.2\cdot2+0.3\cdot10=3.4$, giving probability $e^{-3.4}\approx0.03337$. Using only the starting rate gives $e^{-2(0.5)}\approx0.36788$—a different answer. Raw NHPP waits are generally neither iid nor exponentially distributed with one fixed rate.

06 / Real-data applicationEstimate daily rates; check later daily counts.

Use the same 365 observed 2011 bike-sharing totals. Each count is a completed rental total for one calendar-day exposure, not unmet demand or a measured arrival-time series. A daily Poisson mean is a daily integrated intensity. Many within-day profiles share that integral, so the daily data cannot identify hourly peaks, individual timestamps, or an exponential gap distribution.

Fit only Jan 1–Aug 31: 243 daily counts. This is 243 exposures, unlike Module 6's 242 within-training transition pairs. For independent counts $Y_i\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda e_i)$ with known exposures $e_i$, the likelihood estimate is

$$\widehat\lambda=\frac{\sum_iY_i}{\sum_ie_i},\qquad \widehat\lambda_{\mathrm{daily}}=\frac{802684}{243}=3303.22634\ \text{rentals/day}.$$

Compare a constant daily rate with a calendar rate indexed by the recorded workingday flag. That flag accounts for weekends and holidays; it is not simply a weekday number. Fit each class using only its training days. These estimates define a deterministic rate schedule for later known calendar classes, conditional on the fitted values.

RMSE evaluates point predictions. Mean Poisson negative log-likelihood (NLL, using natural logs and including the log-factorial term) evaluates the assumed predictive law; smaller is better. Pearson residuals compare deviations with the model's proposed standard deviation:

$$\operatorname{NLL}=\frac1{122}\sum_i\left[\mu_i-Y_i\log\mu_i+\log(Y_i!)\right],\qquad r_i=\frac{Y_i-\mu_i}{\sqrt{\mu_i}},\qquad D=\frac1{122}\sum_i r_i^2.$$

Persistence alone specifies no predictive distribution, so its NLL and Pearson score are left blank. The calendar split barely changes these rates and slightly worsens later scores. Both Poisson models severely understate variability: $D$ is around 392–395, far above the scale near one suggested by a correctly specified known Poisson mean.

Why not stop at a pooled variance/mean ratio? Even independent Poisson counts with different deterministic means can be overdispersed when days are pooled. For a randomly selected day, $\operatorname{Var}(Y)=\mathbb E[\mu]+\operatorname{Var}(\mu)$. Here large within-class ratios and ordered later residuals reveal that the two-class model remains inadequate; they do not identify a unique cause. Seasonality, weather, dependence, and omitted demand variation need further investigation.

These are descriptive diagnostics, not an iid hypothesis test. The later period has been reused across modules and is a development holdout, not a fresh final test. No hourly arrivals, causal calendar effects, or calibrated demand uncertainty are established. Dividing a daily rate by 24 supplies only an average hourly rate; using it as a constant intensity adds an unverified assumption.

07 / Verification & interpretationCheck the count law and its assumptions separately.

Computation checks

Check nonnegative rates, units, $N(0)=0$, sorted event times, integer unit jumps, and count means and variances. Compare bin counts with integrated rates and no-event frequencies with exact probabilities. Replay a seed, test a zero rate, and recover the HPP from the flat profile.

Model checks

Define the event and exposure. Audit missing or censored observations. Preserve chronology, compare a baseline, and inspect mean changes, residual scale, and temporal structure. Matching a total count does not establish exponential gaps or independent increments.

Audit this deliberately flawed, instructor-written AI-style suggestion; no AI account is required:

gap = rng.exponential(rate)
p_quiet = np.exp(-rate_at(start) * duration)

Identify the parameterization error, the interval-integral error, the fixed-total and wrong-profile errors, and the future-data leakage.
