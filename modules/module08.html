08 / Renewal processes & continuous-time chains

What changes
while you wait?

The average waiting time is only a beginning. Learn when elapsed time changes the next wait, how a state determines its own clock, and why counting visits can misrepresent time spent in a state.

01 / Practical questionDoes a long wait make the next event more likely?

A component is replaced after a random lifetime. A scheduled service occurs once per hour. A bike station gains a bicycle when a return succeeds and loses one when a rental succeeds. Each system has events, but the information needed to predict its next event differs.

Predict before running

The waiting laws and finite-station model below are synthetic scenarios. We will use the observed bike-sharing dataset to audit which quantities are measured and which would need new data. A daily system-wide rental count is not station inventory.

By the end: simulate ordinary renewal processes, condition a residual wait on age, construct a continuous-time generator, simulate exponential holding times and adjacent-state jumps, compare finite and stationary occupancy, and specify the measurements needed to estimate such a model.

02 / Renewal countsRestart the clock after every renewal.

Let $W_1,W_2,\ldots$ be iid positive cycle lengths with finite mean $\mu$. An ordinary renewal process starts with a renewal at time zero, then uses the same gap law for its first and subsequent cycles. Define

$$S_0=0,\qquad S_n=\sum_{j=1}^nW_j,\qquad N(t)=\max\{n\geq0:S_n\leq t\}.$$

Time zero is the initial renewal, not one of the counted events. A renewal at exactly $t$ is counted. Exponential gaps recover Module 7's homogeneous Poisson process; nonexponential gaps generally remove stationary independent increments. A count alone generally does not supply all the age information needed for future prediction.

The renewal law of large numbers gives $N(t)/t\to1/\mu$ almost surely as $t\to\infty$. The elementary renewal theorem similarly gives $\mathbb E[N(t)]/t\to1/\mu$. Neither says $\mathbb E[N(t)]=t/\mu$ at every finite time. The ordinary process starts at a cycle boundary, not at an arbitrary point inside a cycle.

A non-Poisson benchmark we can calculate exactly

For the Erlang-2 law above, pair successive events of a rate-2 Poisson process. If $M(T)\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(2T)$, then $N(T)=\lfloor M(T)/2\rfloor$. With $T$ measured numerically in hours, this gives

$$\mathbb E[N(T)]=T-\frac{1-e^{-4T}}4,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(N(T))=\frac T2+\frac{1-e^{-8T}}{16}-Te^{-4T}.$$

At eight hours the Erlang count mean is about 7.75 and variance 4.0625. The exponential model gives 8 and 8, while the fixed model gives 8 and 0. A common long-run rate does not imply the same finite count law.

03 / Age & remaining waitCondition on the time already spent waiting.

If $\overline F(w)=\Pr(W>w)$ and the current gap has already lasted $a$ hours, then, provided $\overline F(a)>0$,

$$\Pr(W-a>h\mid W>a)=\frac{\overline F(a+h)}{\overline F(a)}.$$

For an exponential gap this ratio is $e^{-h}$ and does not depend on age. For Erlang-2, $\overline F(w)=e^{-2w}(1+2w)$; after age 0.5, the chance of waiting another 0.5 is about 0.55182, versus 0.60653 for exponential gaps. For a fixed one-hour gap, exactly $1-a$ hours remain. At $h=1-a$ the strict survival probability is zero.

Censoring is information. If observation ends before the next event, you know only that the wait exceeds the observed age. Its likelihood contribution is a survival probability, not a density at that age. Dropping all incomplete gaps biases a naive gap sample toward shorter waits. Starting observation in the middle of a cycle also differs from starting at a renewal.

Age can be added to the state when nonexponential durations matter. A model with state-dependent nonexponential holds is generally a semi-Markov model, beyond the CTMC construction below. Exponential memorylessness is a modeling assumption to check, not a property supplied by a simulation algorithm.

04 / Continuous-time statesGive each state an exit clock.

A finite, time-homogeneous continuous-time Markov chain $X(t)$ has a generator $Q=(q_{ij})$. For distinct states, $q_{ij}\geq0$ is a transition rate, with units inverse time. The diagonal is the negative total exit rate:

$$q_i=-q_{ii}=\sum_{j\ne i}q_{ij},\qquad \sum_jq_{ij}=0,\qquad \Pr(X(t+h)=j\mid X(t)=i)=q_{ij}h+o(h)\quad(j\ne i).$$

$Q$ is not a transition-probability matrix. With row-vector probabilities,

$$P(t)=e^{Qt},\qquad p(t)=p(0)P(t),\qquad p'(t)=p(t)Q.$$

Each row of $P(t)$ is nonnegative and sums to one. The exponential is a matrix exponential, not the entrywise exponential of $Q$. Use scipy.linalg.expm(Q * t) in Python. Unlike $P(t)$, the first-order approximation $I+Qt$ can have negative entries when $t$ is too large.

In state $i$ with $q_i>0$, draw an exponential holding time with mean $1/q_i$, then choose $j\ne i$ with probability $q_{ij}/q_i$. The holding time and next destination are independent conditional on $i$. If $q_i=0$, the state is absorbing. The embedded jump chain has transition probabilities $q_{ij}/q_i$ off the diagonal, but its visit frequencies need not equal continuous-time occupancy.

Renewal versus CTMC: a renewal model uses iid cycle lengths. A CTMC has exponential holds conditional on the current state; successive holds need not share one common rate or be iid after states are ignored. The state supplies the information that determines the next clock.

05 / A finite birth–death modelTrack available bicycles at one synthetic station.

Let $X(t)\in\{0,1,\ldots,K\}$ be the number of available bicycles at a station with $K$ docks. Assume independent, exogenous Poisson return attempts at rate $\alpha$ and rental attempts at rate $\beta$, both per hour. A successful return increases inventory by one; a successful rental decreases it by one. These are scenario assumptions, not estimates from the bundled system-wide data.

$$b_i=\alpha\mathbf1_{\{i<K\}},\qquad d_i=\beta\mathbf1_{\{i>0\}},\qquad q_{i,i+1}=b_i,\quad q_{i,i-1}=d_i,\quad q_{ii}=-(b_i+d_i).$$

Rental attempts at zero and return attempts at $K$ are blocked and leave inventory unchanged. They do not appear as jumps of this state process. At zero the exit rate is $\alpha$; at $K$ it is $\beta$. Empty is not absorbing when returns can occur. All rates are finite, so this finite-state model cannot make infinitely many jumps in finite time.

while time < horizon:
    birth = alpha if state < capacity else 0.0
    death = beta if state > 0 else 0.0
    exit_rate = birth + death
    if exit_rate == 0:
        dwell[state] += horizon - time
        break
    wait = rng.exponential(scale=1 / exit_rate)
    dwell[state] += min(wait, horizon - time)
    if time + wait >= horizon:
        break  # Final censored hold is already counted.
    time += wait
    state += 1 if rng.random() < birth / exit_rate else -1

06 / Time & jump averagesWeight a state by its dwell time.

For positive birth and death rates, the finite chain is irreducible and has a unique stationary law $\pi$. Neighboring flow balance gives $\pi_i b_i=\pi_{i+1}d_{i+1}$. In this constant-rate station model,

$$\rho=\frac\alpha\beta,\qquad \pi_i=\frac{\rho^i}{\sum_{j=0}^{K}\rho^j},\quad i=0,\ldots,K,\qquad \pi Q=0.$$

At $\alpha=\beta$, use the uniform law $\pi_i=1/(K+1)$. The finite chain has a stationary law even when $\alpha\geq\beta$ because its capacity bounds inventory; an infinite-state model needs a separate stability check. Defaults give $\pi=(81,54,36,24,16)/211$, so the long-run empty probability is about 0.38389.

For one path define $D_i(T)=\int_0^T\mathbf1_{\{X(t)=i\}}\,dt$. Include the final interval ending at the observation horizon. Three different summaries are

$$\Pr(X(T)=i)=p_i(T),\qquad \mathbb E\!\left[\frac{D_i(T)}T\right]=\frac1T\int_0^T p_i(t)\,dt,\qquad \lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{D_i(T)}T=\pi_i.$$

A final-time distribution is not the fraction of time spent in a state. Starting with two bicycles, the default 24-hour final distribution is already near stationarity, but the expected fraction of time empty is about 0.37385, below $\pi_0$. Early transient behavior still contributes to the time average.

The long-run jump-visit law is $\widetilde\pi_i=\pi_iq_i/\sum_j\pi_jq_j$. States with faster exit clocks appear more often per unit time in a jump record. Counting visits without their durations overweights those states. Here each jump's destination is counted once, initial states are excluded from that count, and the terminal horizon is not counted as a jump. Finite simulated visit fractions need not equal their long-run target.

Convert occupancy into a blocked-attempt quantity

Under the assumed exogenous Poisson rental attempts, the expected number blocked by an empty station over $[0,T]$ is

$$\mathbb E[B(T)]=\beta\,\mathbb E[D_0(T)],\qquad \frac{\mathbb E[B(T)]}{\mathbb E[A(T)]}=\frac{\mathbb E[D_0(T)]}{T},\quad \mathbb E[A(T)]=\beta T.$$

This ratio of expected counts is not generally $\mathbb E[B(T)/A(T)]$. In the long run, the fraction of rental attempts blocked is $\pi_0$ under the stated attempt model. Stationary successful rental throughput is $\beta(1-\pi_0)$, matching successful returns $\alpha(1-\pi_K)$. No independent confidence interval is formed by treating holds or time grid points within one path as separate replications.

Optional extension · renewal rewards and inspection bias

Attach a reward to each iid renewal cycle. With finite mean cycle length and integrable reward, long-run reward per time is expected cycle reward divided by expected cycle length. The area under cycle age is $W^2/2$, so when $\mathbb E[W^2]<\infty$, long-run time-average age is $\mathbb E[W^2]/(2\mathbb E[W])$. Successive returns to a fixed station state after leaving it also form regenerative cycles in this finite irreducible model; time in another state is a cycle reward. This connects renewal rewards to stationary occupancy. Compare the three gap laws. Explain why a randomly inspected cycle overrepresents long gaps, and distinguish an ordinary process initialized at a renewal from a process observed in equilibrium.
