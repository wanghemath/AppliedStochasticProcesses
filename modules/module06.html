06 / Branching processes

Can a growing population still die out?

A rising expectation can coexist with extinction on most paths. Follow generations of a population, then separate random reproduction, uncertain parameters, and unsupported modeling assumptions.

45–55 min guided work25–35 min notebook practiceConcept Lab 3 · Part B

01 / Practical questionWhat does “growth” tell us about survival?

Imagine a synthetic cascade of tasks. Each task produces a random number of tasks in the next generation, then leaves the active population. There are no external arrivals, resource limits, or interactions between tasks. If each task creates 1.2 successors on average, does the cascade reliably persist?

Predict before running

These populations are synthetic demonstrations. A generation counts parent-to-offspring steps; it is not automatically an hour or a calendar day. Later we will use the observed bike-sharing series to test whether such an interpretation is defensible.

By the end: construct a Galton–Watson process, compare mean growth with finite and eventual extinction, check a simulator against exact calculations, propagate uncertainty about an offspring mean, and identify when aggregate counts do not support an offspring model.

02 / Model & mathematicsCount offspring, not net growth.

Let $Z_n$ be the nonnegative integer population in generation $n$, with $Z_0=z$. Each individual produces offspring $\xi_{n,i}$, independently across individuals and generations, from one fixed distribution. Individuals are replaced by their offspring:

$$Z_{n+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{Z_n}\xi_{n,i},\qquad \Pr(\xi=k)=p_k,\quad p_k\geq0,\quad \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p_k=1.$$

The empty sum is zero: zero is absorbing. A restart after extinction would require immigration or another mechanism. The basic model assumes independent reproduction, identical offspring laws, distinct generations, and no competition, shared shocks, or carrying capacity.

Write $m=\mathbb E[\xi]$ and $\sigma^2=\operatorname{Var}(\xi)$, assumed finite. Conditional on $Z_n$, sums of independent offspring give

$$\mathbb E[Z_{n+1}\mid Z_n]=mZ_n,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(Z_{n+1}\mid Z_n)=\sigma^2 Z_n.$$

Taking expectations yields $\mathbb E[Z_n]=zm^n$. For deterministic $Z_0=z$, let $\mu_n=\mathbb E[Z_n]$ and $v_n=\operatorname{Var}(Z_n)$. The law of total variance gives a stable recursion:

$$\mu_0=z,\quad v_0=0,\qquad \mu_{n+1}=m\mu_n,\quad v_{n+1}=m^2v_n+\sigma^2\mu_n.$$

Thus $m<1$, $m=1$, and $m>1$ describe subcritical, critical, and supercritical mean growth. An expectation averages over all possible populations, including extinct paths and rare large populations. It is not a promise about a single trajectory or its median.

A checkable Poisson offspring model

In the worked experiment $\xi\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(m)$, so $\sigma^2=m$. Adding independent Poisson offspring gives an exact shortcut:

$$Z_{n+1}\mid Z_n\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(mZ_n).$$
path = np.zeros(H + 1, dtype=int)
path[0] = initial_population
for n in range(H):
    path[n + 1] = rng.poisson(m * path[n])
# Keep zeros. Do not add the old population again.

The shortcut samples the same conditional distribution as drawing one offspring count for every parent. Adding $Z_n$ to this draw would define a different model in which parents persist. Capping large populations would also change the distribution and bias mean estimates.

03 / Extinction mathematics“Zero by generation 20” is not eventual extinction.

For one founder, define the offspring probability generating function $f(s)=\mathbb E[s^{\xi}]$ for $0\leq s\leq1$, and let $q_n=\Pr(Z_n=0\mid Z_0=1)$. Independence of descendant families gives

$$q_0=0,\qquad q_{n+1}=f(q_n),\qquad \Pr(Z_n=0\mid Z_0=z)=q_n^z.$$

Because zero is absorbing, being zero at generation $n$ is the same as being extinct by generation $n$. Starting from no founders gives extinction probability 1 at every generation. For positive $z$, $q_n^z$ increases toward the eventual extinction probability $q^z$, where

$$q=\lim_{n\to\infty}q_n=\text{the smallest solution of }f(q)=q\text{ in }[0,1].$$

For a nondegenerate offspring law, $m\leq1$ implies eventual extinction with probability 1. The important exception is $\xi=1$ almost surely: every individual has exactly one descendant, so a positive population never dies out. For $m>1$, $q<1$; it can be zero when zero offspring is impossible. A finite simulation budget cannot establish survival forever.

For Poisson offspring, $f(s)=\exp(m(s-1))$. Here $q=1$ for $m\leq1$ and $0<q<1$ for $m>1$. The supercritical solver brackets the smaller root between 0 and $1/m$. Iterating from zero approaches that root; iterating from one remains at the uninformative fixed point one.

At criticality, the Poisson population becomes extinct almost surely even though its expectation stays at one. At each finite generation, uncommon surviving populations sustain the average. Do not exchange a limit with an expectation without justification.

$$\widehat{\operatorname{MCSE}}(\overline Z_H)=\frac{s_{Z_H}}{\sqrt R},\qquad \widehat{\operatorname{MCSE}}(\widehat p_H)=\sqrt{\frac{\widehat p_H(1-\widehat p_H)}R}.$$

These MCSE formulas use independent full paths. Generations within one path are dependent observations. A small simulated set may miss rare large populations; a zero estimated error at an all-zero or all-one event fraction does not prove the exact probability is at the endpoint. The display also reports MCSEs from the exact model variance and extinction probability. Monte Carlo noise can put the simulated extinct fraction above the exact eventual probability; the exact finite-generation curve cannot exceed that limit. These are numerical errors conditional on the stated model, not uncertainty about whether the model describes reality.

05 / Parameter uncertaintyCarry the uncertainty through the extinction calculation.

Connect to Module 5 using a separate, hand-built synthetic offspring sample: 20 parents with offspring counts consisting of six zeros, eight ones, four twos, and two threes. The total is 22 offspring. These records were created for instruction; they are not bike-sharing observations or measured cascade data.

Assume these parent-level counts are iid Poisson($m$). With a Gamma prior in shape–rate notation,

$$m\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha=2,\beta=2),\qquad L(m)\propto m^{22}e^{-20m},\qquad m\mid\text{synthetic counts}\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(24,22).$$

The posterior mean is $24/22\approx1.09091$, with a 95% interval about $[0.69897,1.56870]$. Direct Gamma draws are available, so MCMC is unnecessary here. The inference assumes independent, completely observed offspring records; unrecorded parents or unfinished reproduction would require another likelihood.

For $z$ founders and a shared unknown $m$, integrate the conditional extinction probability:

$$\Pr(Z_H=0\mid\text{synthetic counts},Z_0=z)=\mathbb E_{m\mid\text{data}}[q_H(m)^z].$$

For an entire future population, draw one $m$ and keep it fixed for all generations and all founder families. The families are independent conditional on $m$; after integrating shared parameter uncertainty, they need not be independent. Therefore integrate $q_H(m)^z$, not $(\mathbb E[q_H(m)])^z$. Redrawing $m$ each generation defines a random-environment model.

For one founder and $H=20$, integrated extinction is about 0.78655 versus 0.81337 at the posterior mean. The direct posterior calculation averages exact conditional probabilities rather than adding another layer of binary path simulation. This conditional Monte Carlo approach reduces variance at the same number of parameter draws. The direction of the integrated-versus-plug-in difference is not universal: try three founders. The posterior probability that $m\leq1$ is about 0.36258; it is not itself the eventual extinction probability, because supercritical populations can also become extinct.

Three different uncertainties: offspring randomness varies future paths at a fixed $m$; posterior uncertainty concerns the unknown $m$; MCSE measures error in numerically averaging posterior quantities. More simulation reduces the last, not the first two. A posterior interval for $m$ is not a predictive interval for a future population.

06 / Real-data applicationDo daily rentals behave like generations?

The bundled UCI series records completed rentals per calendar day. It provides no parent–offspring links, generation labels, cascade identifiers, or evidence that today's rentals create tomorrow's rentals. Weather, commuting schedules, seasonality, repeat riders, and external arrivals do not appear as iid reproduction in the basic model.

Nevertheless, a provisional conditional count model is a useful falsifiable diagnostic. Let $X_t$ be the recorded daily total and tentatively write $X_{t+1}\mid X_t\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(mX_t)$. For the 242 adjacent pairs wholly within Jan 1–Aug 31, the conditional Poisson likelihood gives

$$\widehat m=\frac{\sum_{t=0}^{241}X_{t+1}}{\sum_{t=0}^{241}X_t}=\frac{801699}{797626}\approx1.005106403.$$

This is a fitted conditional multiplier under a proposed model. It does not identify an actual offspring mechanism. An average of day-to-day ratios is a different estimator. Calling the fitted number a reproductive rate, or using it to predict extinction of bike sharing, is unsupported.

Freeze this multiplier. For each September–December target day, predict its conditional mean from the actual preceding day's count. The first forecast uses Aug 31 to predict Sep 1; that boundary pair is not part of the fit. These are rolling one-step predictions, not one long autonomous forecast. Compare with the simple persistence prediction $\widehat X_{t+1}=X_t$.

Under the provisional model, the conditional variance equals its mean. Define Pearson residuals using the fixed training estimate:

$$r_{t+1}=\frac{X_{t+1}-\widehat m X_t}{\sqrt{\widehat m X_t}},\qquad D=\frac1{122}\sum_{\text{later targets}}r_{t+1}^2.$$

The observed value $D\approx440.34$ exposes variability far beyond the proposed Poisson scale. RMSE is about 985.53 rentals/day, slightly worse than persistence at 982.42. These are descriptive development diagnostics, not an iid test: days remain ordered and dependent, and the later period has already been examined in earlier modules. A multiplier slightly above one supplies neither evidence of a branching mechanism nor a credible long-run growth forecast.

What would make a branching application credible? Define a generation and offspring unit, record parent or cascade links, document observation of zero offspring and incomplete follow-up, and check independence, stable reproduction, immigration, and population limits. Until then, retain the rental count and Markov baselines for the transportation project.

07 / Verification & interpretationCheck a simulator separately from a model.

Computation checks

Confirm integer nonnegative populations, absorbing zero, correct mean and variance recursions, and extinction frequencies near $q_H^z$. Test zero founders, zero offspring mean, critical Poisson, and deterministic one offspring. Replay seeds. Report the horizon and keep every path.

Model checks

Define what counts as a parent, offspring, and generation. Separate observed records from assumptions. Preserve the fitting boundary, compare a baseline, and inspect residual variation. A correct simulator cannot establish an offspring mechanism in aggregate data.

Audit this deliberately flawed, instructor-written AI-style suggestion. No AI account is needed:

m = all_daily_counts[1:].sum() / all_daily_counts[:-1].sum()
for n in range(H):
    population = min(rng.poisson(m * population), 100)
mean_population = samples[samples > 0].mean()
print("Every path alive at H survives forever")

It clips growth, removes extinct paths, uses later outcomes in fitting, and mistakes finite-horizon survival for survival forever. Repair the update, stored outcomes, fitting boundary, and interpretation; then compare with exact finite-generation benchmarks. Record consequential AI assistance and your independent checks; “No AI used” is valid.
